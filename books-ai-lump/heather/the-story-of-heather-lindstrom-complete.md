# Scales, Tails, and Safe People

## Saturday Pretzel Day, The Empty Shelf, & The Boy Without a Phone Bridge

### Part 1: The Empty Pretzel Table, The Oak Frame Off the Shelf, & Why Wesley Blew Up

**Date:** 2003-01-11 at 10:45 America/New_York

#### Saturday, January 11, 2003, 10:45 AM — Aunt Linda's Living Room in Chesapeake: Two Taps on the Front Door & The Question Heather Dreaded All Week

![On a sunlit Saturday morning in January 2003 inside Aunt Linda's Chesapeake split-level living room, fifteen-year-old non-verbal Wesley Collins in a navy-blue fleece quarter-zip pullover and blue jeans with his blue silicone ring on his wrist cradles the oak-framed Emerald Creek Mall snapshot protectively inward against his chest while pointing emphatically toward the closed front door in heartbroken teenage fury, as seventeen-year-old Heather Collins sits stricken and remorseful on the bottom stair step beside her car keys and Aunt Linda watches quietly from the oak bookshelf.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/heather/e001/jan_2003_chesapeake_living_room_wesley_photo_blowout_heather.jpg)

*Thump-thump-thump-thump.*

Seventeen-year-old Heather Collins froze halfway down the carpeted stairs of her Aunt Linda's split-level living room, her fingers tightening around her keyring until the brass teeth bit into her palm.

Coming eagerly down the upper landing behind her—already dressed twenty minutes early in his favorite unbranded navy-blue fleece quarter-zip pullover, clean blue jeans, and freshly tied black athletic sneakers, with his smooth solid-blue silicone sensory ring slipped neatly over his right wrist—was her fifteen-year-old cousin Wesley.

Every Saturday morning of the month followed a clockwork rhythm Wesley trusted more than sunrise, and the second Saturday after New Year's Day was the crown jewel of his calendar. Already tall for fifteen with short sandy-brown hair brushed neatly across his forehead by his mom, Wesley bounded down the last three beige carpeted steps, crossed the sunlit living room in four long strides, and tapped his palm twice—*pat, pat*—against the white-painted wood of the front door. Turning around with a huge, radiant grin lighting up his whole face, Wesley lifted his right fist in front of his chest and tapped his own knuckles with his left hand, mimicking the exact peer-to-peer knuckle bump sixteen-year-old Nate Prairie always greeted him with beside the glass elevator up on the second floor of Emerald Creek Mall.

*"Mm-hm! Hmph-hm!"* Wesley hummed brightly, pointing from the front door straight to the car keys in Heather's hand.

Heather's stomach dropped like an elevator cable snapping in midair.

All week long—ever since Monday morning at seven-forty-two outside Mr. Alderman's computer lab in Room 204 at Northwood High School, when senior football player Tyler's smirking rumor in Homeroom 212 had set her cheeks on fire and she had screamed at Nate in front of the glass trophy cases without letting Nate or Katie finish two sentences—Heather had kept telling herself she was the injured party. All Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday, whenever she caught a glimpse of Nate's and Katie's black motorized SwiftChairs rolling quietly side by side down B-Wing in dead, shuttered silence without even glancing her way, Heather had wrapped her pride tighter around her shoulders like armor, convincing herself that walking away first had saved her dignity.

Not once in five days had she braced herself for Saturday morning at ten-forty-five.

Over by the tall oak bookshelf between the front windows, forty-four-year-old Aunt Linda paused with a folded dish towel in her hands, looking gently from Wesley's beaming face to Heather's pale cheeks. Aunt Linda already knew; Heather's mom had mentioned the breakup quietly over the kitchen phone on Wednesday night, and Aunt Linda had suggested letting Heather tell Wesley gently in person rather than ambushing him before breakfast.

"Hey, Wes," Heather said, trying to make her voice sound breezy and bright as she took one slow step down onto the bottom stair tread. "Listen, bud—you and I can still drive down to Great Bridge for French fries or look at the turtles at the pet shop on Battlefield Boulevard if you want, okay? Just the two of us today."

Wesley's happy grin faltered half an inch. His sandy eyebrows pulled together in confusion. Stepping two paces away from the front door toward the oak bookshelf, he pointed squarely at the five-by-seven wooden picture frame resting in the place of honor on the middle shelf—the candid snapshot Aunt Linda had taken fourteen months earlier in November of 2001 beneath the white tented ceiling cones of Emerald Creek Mall, showing Katie and Nate parked side by side in their SwiftChairs across the table from Heather and Wesley over four red plastic trays of warm cinnamon-sugar pretzel nuggets from Annie's Pretzels.

Wesley tapped his index finger twice near the edge of the frame, bumped his own knuckles together a second time, and looked expectantly back at Heather with a questioning upward hum. *Nate? Katie? Ten-fifty mall time?*

Heather swallowed past a dry, aching lump in her throat and sank slowly down onto the bottom carpeted stair step, setting her car keys on the beige pile beside her hip because her fingers were suddenly shaking.

"We... we aren't meeting Nate and Katie at Annie's Pretzels today, Wes," Heather said quietly, looking down at her knees before forcing herself to meet her cousin's eyes. "Nate and I aren't boyfriend and girlfriend anymore. We broke up on Monday. So we won't be hanging out with Nate and Katie anymore."

For three full seconds, the Chesapeake living room was so quiet Heather could hear the furnace click on beneath the floorboards.

Wesley stood completely motionless in the slant of pale January sunshine. Heather watched every single word register behind his brown eyes in slow, devastating sequence—watched the eager Saturday light extinguish all at once as his brain connected *not boyfriend and girlfriend anymore* to *no Emerald Creek Mall*, to *no cinnamon pretzel nuggets split evenly across the red tray*, to *no sixteen-year-old guy in a black wheelchair rolling up beside him, bumping his knuckles like an equal, and never once talking down to him like a baby*.

Then Wesley's face flushed scarlet from his collar to the roots of his sandy hair, and a sound tore out of his chest that Heather had never once heard him direct at her in all fifteen years of his life.

*"AAH—NO! HNN-GH!"*

Heather flinched back against the stair riser in pure shock as Wesley threw his left arm straight out toward the closed front door, his whole tall teenage frame trembling from head to sneaker soles with volcanic, heartbroken fury. He didn't come within six feet of her—Wesley had never raised a hand to another living soul in his life—yet the sheer volume and raw betrayal roaring out of his throat hit Heather harder than a slap across the face.

"Wes, wait—please don't yell, it wasn't my fault!" Heather stammered, tears springing hot into her hazel eyes as she held both hands palms-up from the bottom step. "Someone at school told everybody that Nate was at a bad party on New Year's Eve, and people in my homeroom were laughing at me, so I had to—"

*"UH-UH! NO!"* Wesley bellowed right over her excuse, shaking his head violently from side to side and stamping one sneaker heel straight down into the carpet where he stood. Even without spoken sentences, every line of Wesley's posture screamed what he thought of that story. Wesley knew Nate Prairie. Wesley had sat across from Nate at Annie's Pretzels a dozen Saturdays in a row; Wesley knew the quiet, gentle boy who always waited patiently when the mall crowd got loud, who slid the biggest pretzel nugget onto Wesley's napkin, and who reached sideways without thinking to hold his twin sister Katie's hand whenever an escalator motor buzzed too sharply.

Before Heather could utter another word, a sudden look of sheer panic crossed Wesley's tear-streaked face.

He spun on his heel toward the oak bookshelf—remembering, from a hundred prime-time television shows he had watched from the sofa, what angry teenage girls always did to photographs of ex-boyfriends after a breakup. Reaching out with both hands before anyone could touch it, Wesley lifted Aunt Linda's five-by-seven oak picture frame clean off the middle shelf, turned the glass face strictly inward against the navy fleece over his own sternum so nobody in the room could even look at his picture of Nate, and pointed one rigid, trembling finger back toward the front door in a final furious indictment of his cousin.

"Wesley, honey, Heather would never touch your picture," Aunt Linda said calmly and softly from beside the bookcase, keeping her hands relaxed at her sides so she didn't crowd him. "That frame belongs to you, sweetheart. You can keep it safe in your room."

Cradling the wooden back of the frame tight against his chest with one arm while his blue silicone ring pressed against the oak border, Wesley shot Heather one last glistening, devastated glare—the look of a fifteen-year-old boy who realized the person he trusted most had just blown up the only friendship he had of his own—pivoted wide around the opposite side of the banister so he wouldn't even brush past Heather's knees on the bottom step, pounded straight up the carpeted stairs two at a time, and slammed his bedroom door shut with a boom that rattled every framed family portrait down the upper hall.

#### Saturday, January 11, 2003, 11:02 AM — The Empty Spot on the Bookshelf & What It Means to Have Zero Bridge of Your Own

Down at the foot of the stairs, Heather sat frozen on the bottom step in her cream cable-knit sweater, both hands clapped over her mouth as hot, silent tears spilled between her fingers.

Upstairs behind Wesley's closed bedroom door, there wasn't another shout—only the muffled, rhythmic creak of Wesley's bedsprings as he rocked back and forth on the edge of his mattress, trying all by himself to press his frayed nerves back together around a five-by-seven piece of glass.

Aunt Linda walked quietly across the beige carpet, looked for a second at the bare rectangle of dustless oak on the bookshelf where the mall photograph had stood since November of 2001, and sat down gently on the third stair step just above Heather's shoulder. She didn't scold Heather, and she didn't tell her that everything was fine.

"He—he's never yelled at me like that in his whole life, Aunt Linda," Heather whispered thickly, wiping her wet cheeks with the ribbed cuff of her sweater sleeve. "Never once. Not when he had pneumonia in fifth grade, not when those middle-school boys laughed at him at the park. He looked at me like he hated me."

"He doesn't hate you, Heather," Aunt Linda said quietly, resting a warm, steady hand between Heather's shoulder blades. "He's terrified. And right now, your cousin is grieving a loss he has zero power to fix by himself."

Heather blinked up through blurry lashes. "Because he liked eating pretzels at the mall?"

Aunt Linda shook her head slowly, her expression gentle and unflinchingly honest at the same time.

"Think about Wesley's Monday-through-Friday world for a minute, sweetheart," Aunt Linda said softly. "You're seventeen. You have a Virginia driver's license, your dad's car keys sitting right there on the step, a telephone in your kitchen you can speak into whenever you want, an instant-messaging screen name, and six periods a day full of kids who talk to you at your locker. If you have a fight with a friend at Northwood High, you have twenty different ways to make a new friend by Friday afternoon."

Aunt Linda glanced up toward the quiet ceiling above the stairwell.

"How many friends Wesley's own age—boys who aren't paid special-education aides, adult cousins, or teachers—has Wesley ever had in fifteen years?" Aunt Linda asked quietly.

Heather opened her mouth, and nothing came out.

"Just one," Heather whispered after three seconds, her chest tightening until it hurt to breathe. "Just Nate."

"Just Nate," Aunt Linda confirmed softly. "A sixteen-year-old boy who never once stared when Wesley hummed, never once talked over Wesley's head to you like Wesley was invisible, and treated him like a regular guy buddy from the very first Saturday at that food court. And think about how Wesley sees what just happened this morning, Heather. Wesley can't pick up our kitchen landline and dial the Prairie house in Kempsville to say, *Hey Nate, I'm still your friend even if Heather broke up with you.* Wesley doesn't have an email address. Wesley can't drive across Chesapeake or ride two city buses out to Military Highway on his own. You were his entire bridge to Nate and Katie—the only bridge he had."

Heather stared down at her brass car keys glinting on the beige carpet pile, and for the very first time since Lauren Mitchell had giggled behind her manicure file in Homeroom 212 on Monday morning, the self-righteous armor Heather had worn all week cracked straight down the center.

Outside Room 204 on Monday morning, Nate had reached out from his wheelchair with confusion in his blue eyes trying to explain, and Katie had wheeled right up beside her twin brother asking Heather just to listen for thirty seconds before classes started. Heather hadn't given them thirty seconds. She hadn't even given them ten. Stung by teenage embarrassment in front of kids who didn't care two cents about her or Wesley, she had shouted Nate down in a public hallway, spun on her heel, and slammed the door on both of them—taking fifteen-year-old Wesley's only friend in the world down with her without even pausing once to think about her cousin.

"Should I go upstairs and try to explain?" Heather whispered miserably.

"Give him an hour with his picture first," Aunt Linda said gently, patting Heather's knee as she stood up. "And Heather? If there's even a chance you reacted too fast on Monday without hearing that boy out, don't wait six months to swallow your pride. Trust takes a long time to build with people who guard their hearts—and Wesley already knows how rare safe people are."

### Part 2: Two Taps on the Glass Every Saturday & Five Months of Frozen Pride

**Date:** 2003-02-15 at 10:15 America/New_York

![On a sunlit winter morning inside Aunt Linda's Chesapeake split-level living room, fifteen-year-old non-verbal Wesley Collins cradles the oak-framed Emerald Creek Mall snapshot protectively against his chest while pointing emphatically toward the front door as seventeen-year-old Heather Collins looks on in quiet remorse.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/heather/e001/jan_2003_chesapeake_living_room_wesley_photo_blowout_heather.jpg)

#### Saturday, February 15, 2003, 10:15 AM — Five Weeks Since the Blowout, Cousin Hannah's Green Parka, & Wesley's Silent Protest

*Tap. Tap.*

Five Saturdays after the morning Wesley had snatched the oak frame off the living-room bookshelf and slammed his bedroom door, the empty rectangle on Aunt Linda's middle shelf was still empty—and fifteen-year-old Wesley still hadn't let Heather off the hook for a single weekend.

Standing squarely in front of the kitchen table where Heather sat picking at a cinnamon-raisin bagel in her maroon Northwood Lions sweatshirt, Wesley held the five-by-seven oak frame angled carefully toward his own chest, turned it just thirty degrees toward Heather's eyes for two deliberate seconds so she had to look straight at sixteen-year-old Nate and Katie smiling across the Annie's Pretzels trays, and tapped his knuckle twice against the corner of the wood before pointing a steady, unmistakable finger down the hallway toward Heather's parked car in the driveway.

*"Mm-hm,"* Wesley insisted in a low, urgent hum—not shouting the way he had on January eleventh, just fixing his older cousin with an earnest, unyielding teenage stare that asked the exact same question he had asked every Saturday for five straight weeks: *Go say sorry. Today.*

This Saturday morning, Heather couldn't even look Wesley in the eye—because four days earlier at Tuesday lunch inside the Northwood High cafeteria, her last shred of self-justification had burned to ash right in front of two hundred students.

Sitting three tables down from senior football player Tyler on Tuesday afternoon, Heather had watched Tyler lean across the varsity aisle with that exact same practiced, confidential smirk he had used in January, tell senior girls' soccer captain Kristen Bauer that he had personally spotted her boyfriend Gavin Whitfield hugging *"some pretty redhead in a forest-green parka"* behind the movie theater at Kempsville Plaza on Saturday night, and immediately tack on a sleazy pitch to dump Gavin and let Tyler take her out for Valentine's Day instead. Just as Heather had done in Homeroom 212 back on January sixth, Kristen immediately laughed and called Tyler an idiot, telling him that the redhead in the forest-green parka was Gavin's nineteen-year-old cousin Hannah from Virginia Dominion University whose tire had blown out behind the cinema. And right on cue, stung at being called an idiot, Tyler fired the exact same second-stage double-down lie that had shattered Heather's trust in January—scoffing *"Keep telling yourself that, Bauer!"* and swearing up and down that he knew what Gavin's cousin looked like, that this was some totally different redhead, and that Kristen should ditch him and go out with Tyler on Friday night.

Except where Heather had crumbled in embarrassment the second Tyler doubled down, Kristen Bauer—and her entire soccer table—hadn't budged an inch. Without blinking, Kristen nailed Tyler's bluff straight into the cafeteria floor loud enough for four tables to hear—pointing out that Tyler had never met Cousin Hannah in his life, that Kristen had helped Gavin's mother pick out that forest-green parka for Hannah's birthday at Emerald Creek Mall, and that Kristen had been on three-way kitchen speakerphone with Gavin's mother and Hannah while Gavin bolted her spare tire on—before her varsity teammates laughed Tyler right out of his chair for running the exact same sleazebag breakup scam on every girl with a boyfriend whom he wanted to date, just like he had already tried on two girls on varsity track back in October.

Sitting frozen over her untouched lunch tray on Tuesday afternoon as her metal spoon clattered onto the linoleum, Heather had felt every drop of blood drain out of her face until the cafeteria lights swam—because suddenly she remembered Thursday, January ninth, three mornings after she had screamed at Nate's locker, when Tyler had leaned over her homeroom desk smelling of cheap cologne asking if he could take her out Friday night *"so a real guy could treat her right."* Even though she had brushed Tyler off in disgust that morning, she hadn't connected the dots until right then in the cafeteria.

*Katie's straight blonde hair.*

Six feet across the aisle at Table Twelve sat Nate and Katie side by side in their black SwiftChairs—with zero mystery blonde party girl anywhere in Nate's life across five straight weeks of winter classes—eating dry toasted oat cereal and drinking green cans of ginger ale, the exact two things Nate had begged Heather to believe he and Katie had eaten behind that locked bedroom door during their cousin Brittany's unauthorized New Year's Eve party. On January sixth in Homeroom 212, Heather's very first instinct—*That was his twin sister Katie, you idiot!*—had been one hundred percent right. Tyler hadn't exposed a betrayal; furious that pretty Heather Collins was dating Nate instead of him, Tyler had glimpsed blonde-haired Katie from behind on New Year's Eve and run his sleazy, copy-paste breakup scam on Heather hoping to get in her good graces on the rebound.

And unlike Kristen Bauer and the track girls, who had seen straight through a sleazebag's pickup routine and stood by the boys they loved, Heather had let two minutes of homeroom pride outweigh eighteen months of knowing Nathaniel Prairie's heart.

"I know, Wes," Heather whispered miserably at Aunt Linda's kitchen table, resting her forehead in one palm while Wesley stood patiently beside her chair holding his oak frame and his blue silicone ring. "You were right about Nate the whole time. Tyler made the whole thing up."

Wesley's posture straightened instantly, hope flaring bright across his face as he tapped the oak frame once more and gestured eagerly toward Heather's jacket hung over the chair back. *Then let's go right now!*

Heather reached out to touch her jacket sleeve—and then her hand froze halfway there as a cold wave of seventeen-year-old cowardice knotted her stomach all over again.

Every single weekday morning for the past five weeks, whenever Heather passed Stairway A at Northwood High where Mr. Henderson's short yellow Bus 417 lowered its hydraulic lift onto the sidewalk, or whenever she passed Room 204 before first block, she had seen what Katie Prairie's blue eyes looked like now. Katie wasn't glaring at Heather or whispering about her to classmates; to Katie and Nate, Heather Collins had simply ceased to exist—walled off behind three inches of bulletproof glass so she could never hurt Nate a second time. Walking straight up to their two wheelchairs in a crowded Monday hallway and admitting out loud that she had screamed at Nate over a lie she hadn't even bothered to check felt so terrifyingly humiliating that Heather's throat closed up just picturing it.

"I can't just drive over to their house unannounced in February, Wes, it's too awkward right now," Heather rationalized weakly, pulling her hand back into her lap and watching the bright spark in her cousin's brown eyes dim right back into disappointed disbelief. "Give it a little time for things to cool down at school first, okay? Come spring—maybe around graduation—and especially before Mom and Dad finish the paperwork on moving us back home to Minnesota at the end of June, I promise I'll walk up and tell Nate and Katie I'm sorry."

Wesley stared at her for four long, quiet seconds. Slowly, deliberately, he shook his head once—telling her without a single spoken syllable that waiting was a mistake—turned the glass of his cherished mall photograph safely back against his navy fleece pullover, and carried it back upstairs to its permanent guard post on his bedroom nightstand.
