# Episode 1: Past the Back Fence

## Chapter 1: First Night Without Shouting

### Part 1: Five Miles Past the Wire

**Date:** 2000-06-20 at 16:30 America/Los_Angeles

![Eighteen-year-old triplets Luke, Tessa, and Sadie Bridger sitting close together by a warm stone keyhole campfire in front of their blue-and-white dome tent beside a clear mountain stream under twilight Colville National Forest pines, as Sadie fingerpicks Grandpa Owen's vintage spruce-top acoustic guitar.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/cedar-run/e001/jun_2000_cedar_run_night_one_campfire_tent.jpg)

#### 4:30 PM — The Boundary Wire

The bottom strand of rusted barbed wire groaned under my boot heel as I held the middle wire up by a weathered tamarack post so Tessa and Sadie could duck through with Grandpa Owen's guitar case.

Down through the last fifty yards of second-growth pine behind us, the back screen door of our parents' house slammed hard enough to rattle the kitchen windowpane. My father's voice carried halfway up the dry needle slope—one final barked warning not to bother coming up the gravel drive until all three of us were ready to grow up, act like normal adults, and go our separate ways—and then the deadbolt clicked home and the afternoon woods swallowed the house whole.

Nobody cried. Nobody even looked back down the hill.

Sadie straightened up on the public-forest side of the wire, adjusted the padded strap of her canvas external-frame pack, and checked the strip of gray duct tape holding the bottom latch shut on the battered black hardshell guitar case. A stray curl of chestnut hair had worked loose over her temple above her navy-and-white striped shirt, catching the late June sun. Across from her, with the exact same gray-hazel eyes and sun-freckled nose and her hair tied half-back with a leather thong, Tessa hitched the waist belt of her tan work vest, tested the weight of the rolled blue-and-white dome tent lashed beneath her pack frame, and blew out a long, slow breath.

"Eighteen," Tessa said quietly.

"Eighteen," I answered, letting the barbed wire spring back into place between its rusted staples before stepping over it myself.

For as long as any of the three of us could remember, the crime in that house behind us had been the simplest thing in the world: we actually liked being together. When we were eight, our mother had demanded the elementary school put all three of us in separate classrooms so we would stop finishing each other's sentences at supper. When we were fourteen and spent an entire July rebuilding Grandpa Owen's cedar garden shed side by side instead of signing up for three different summer camps in three different counties, our father had lectured us for two hours at the dining table about unhealthy codependence and spreading our wings. By seventeen, every slamming cupboard door and raised voice through the drywall came back to the same ultimatum—normal brothers and sisters couldn't wait to get away from each other after graduation, and the minute we turned eighteen we were going onto separate tracks whether we liked it or not.

This morning, over cold toast on our eighteenth birthday, we had told them calmly that wherever we went next, the three of us were renting one roof and figuring it out together.

By four o'clock our packs were on the back porch steps, our birth certificates and Social Security cards were zipped inside Sadie's waterproof map pouch alongside four hundred and twelve dollars in mowed-lawn and stacked-cordwood cash, and my father was pointing down the driveway toward the county road.

Only we hadn't walked down the driveway toward the highway. Five acres behind the woodshed lay a million acres of Colville National Forest—ancient granite ridges, western larch, ponderosa pine, and nameless cold creeks climbing into the Kettle River Range with not a highway or neighbor in sight. We had walked these lower deer trails since we were ten years old whenever the shouting inside got too loud to think.

"How far in do you want to go?" Sadie asked, shifting Grandpa Owen's 1974 Clearwater guitar to her left hand so her right fingers brushed my flannel sleeve.

I looked upslope past the orange-barked ponderosas toward the high green notch where the old nineteen-sixties logging spur had washed out during the spring melt six years ago. Beyond that collapsed culvert, no pickup truck or four-wheeler could climb a hundred yards, and we had never once seen another bootprint in the silt.

"Past the washout," I said. "Deep enough that we can't hear a single engine off the county road. Let's just get camp up before dark and let our ears stop ringing."

Tessa reached out with her free hand and caught Sadie's wrist and mine together for one second—three wrists touching, leather cord against woven thread against flannel cuff—before turning uphill onto the deer trace.

"Lead on, brother," Tessa said.

***

#### 7:45 PM — Beyond the Culvert

Five miles uphill with forty-pound canvas packs and a hardshell guitar case sorted out what mattered fast.

Around six o'clock we scrambled down and up the steep, fern-choked ravine where winter runoff had torn the old logging grade clean out to bare granite bedrock. Above the washout, the abandoned spur narrowed to twin ribbons of pinegrass and wild strawberry runners winding beneath towering western red cedars whose trunks smelled warm and spicy in the slant golden light. There were no mountain peaks shaped like cones anywhere in this corner of northeast Washington—only long, rounded granite shoulders furred green with Douglas fir and airy, bright-needled tamarack larches stretching fold after fold toward the Kettle Crest.

Half an hour before sundown, the sound of water brought us down off the bench into a hidden flat tucked inside an elbow of smooth gray granite boulders.

A stream fifteen feet wide came tumbling out of a narrow cedar gorge upstream, dropping over a knee-high ledge into a deep, slow-moving pool the color of dark bottle glass before chattering away over clean pale gravel. Twenty paces back from the water, well above the spring drift-line of bleached alder twigs, lay a dry carpet of russet pine needles sheltered between two massive ponderosas.

"Right here," Sadie breathed, setting the guitar case gently atop a flat boulder as if laying down a sleeping child. "Feel the air? Cold off the water, warm under the trees—zero mosquitoes."

We didn't have to assign jobs; eighteen years of working with six hands meant everybody already knew the rhythm. While Sadie unpacked the scarred enamel kettle and filled it where the stream poured clear and icy over clean granite lip, Tessa snapped the shock-corded fiberglass poles of our old two-person dome tent together and staked all four corners taut into the firm mineral soil beneath the pine duff. I dropped my pack, slipped Grandpa Owen's leather-sheathed hatchet and canvas tool roll out from under the top flap, and gathered dead, wrist-thick lower pine branches that snapped crisp as rifle shots—never damp wood off the ground—stacking them beside a ring of dry creek stones dug down to bare sand twenty feet downwind of the tent door.

One strike of the ferrocerium rod against the spine of my pocketknife threw a shower of white-hot sparks into a bird's nest of shredded dry cedar inner bark. A curl of blue smoke rose, caught, and licked up into clean orange flame.

By the time full twilight settled purple between the tree trunks, the kettle had come to a rolling boil for three minutes to make doubly sure of the mountain water, and Tessa was dealing out flour tortillas smeared thick with peanut butter and sprinkled with raisins from a zip-top bag while herbal peppermint tea cooled in three tin mugs.

We sat shoulder to shoulder on a fallen cedar bole facing the keyhole fire—Tessa on my right with her boots kicked off and her wool-socked feet warming near the warm stones, Sadie on my left with her mug cradled between both palms.

For three full minutes, nobody spoke a word.

Out in the dark beyond our tiny circle of amber light, the creek kept up a steady, low, braided rush over granite. High overhead, a light night breeze moved through a hundred-foot ceiling of pine needles with a sound like distant surf. Nobody slammed a bedroom door. Nobody shouted up a staircase asking why all three of us were sitting in the same room again. Nobody told Sadie to put Grandpa Owen's guitar away and go fill out housing forms five hundred miles from her brother and sister.

My shoulders dropped two full inches before I even realized I had been holding them up around my ears since breakfast.

"Listen to that," Tessa whispered against my right shoulder.

"Listen to what?" I asked softly.

"Nothing," she said, letting her head rest against my collarbone with a long, shaky exhale that turned into a quiet laugh. "Absolutely nothing. Nobody yelling at all."

Sadie set her tin mug down on a flat stone, unlatched the black guitar case, and lifted Grandpa Owen's 1974 Clearwater dreadnought onto her knee. The aged Sitka spruce soundboard glowed warm honey-gold in the firelight; up at the top of the neck, the plain dark rosewood headstock—stripped bare years ago when Grandpa Owen sanded and hand-oiled the neck himself—caught a tiny glint of orange on its six nickel tuning pegs.

She didn't strum loud. She just thumb-picked a slow, rolling pattern in open G, warm and mellow as an old nineteen-seventy-five record spinning in Grandpa's workshop while rain tapped the tin roof. After four bars, Tessa hummed a low alto third underneath the melody without needing words, and my chest vibrated as I found the baritone root note an octave down—three voices locking into place as effortlessly as breathing.

When the embers burned down to cherry-red coals under gray ash and the mountain air bit crisp through our flannels, we banked the fire ring safe with sand and crawled inside the blue-and-white dome tent together.

A tent meant for two campers took some choreography for three eighteen-year-olds, except we had slept piled together through thunderstorms since we were toddlers. We unzipped both old rectangular sleeping bags flat—one underneath us as a quilted mattress over our folded canvas packs, the second pulled wide across all three of us like a shared comforter. I stretched out on my back in the middle; Tessa curled onto her side tucked warm under my right arm with her forehead against my shoulder, and Sadie tucked right in under my left arm with her fingers resting across my ribs, both sisters breathing in slow, identical rhythm within two minutes.

Looking straight up through the mesh roof vent under the rainfly, I could see three sharp white stars framed between black ponderosa boughs. My legs ached from the climb, we had no roof thicker than nylon, and we owned four hundred and twelve dollars in the entire world.

It was, without a single close second, the happiest night of my life.

### Part 2: Snowmelt and Red Cedar

**Date:** 2000-06-21 at 08:45 America/Los_Angeles

![Folded denim jeans, faded olive flannel, cream henley, navy-striped shirt, and leather work boots stacked neatly dry on a sun-warmed gray granite boulder beside a misty emerald mountain creek pool in Colville National Forest, while Luke, Tessa, and Sadie Bridger laugh collarbone-deep in the brisk snowmelt water.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/cedar-run/e001/jun_2000_cedar_run_morning_creek_pool_folded_clothes_boulder.jpg)

#### 8:45 AM — Waking in the Quiet

Sunlight woke us—not an alarm clock, not knuckles hammering on hollow-core bedroom doors, just warm gold filtering through green pine needles and painting bright dappled coins across the blue nylon tent wall.

For ten seconds I lay completely still in the middle of the unzipped sleeping bags, listening to the bright chatter of a red-breasted nuthatch somewhere up the trunk above us and the steady rush of cold water over granite. Tessa was still fast asleep with one arm slung across my chest and her chin tucked into the crook of my right shoulder; on my left, Sadie was already awake, propped slightly on one elbow watching a shaft of dusty morning sun move across the tent zipper with a look of quiet wonder on her face.

She caught me looking at her and smiled—barely a breath above a whisper so she wouldn't jolt her twin.

"Morning," Sadie murmured.

"Morning," I whispered back. "Sleep okay?"

"Nine straight hours," she whispered, resting her cheek back down against my left shoulder. "I didn't wake up once with my stomach in knots."

On my right, Tessa stirred, blinked her gray-hazel eyes against the bright canvas glow, and stretched both legs down to the foot of the tent until her toes bumped the nylon seam. She paused, listened to the forest for three heartbeats, and grinned without even lifting her head.

"Still nobody screaming," Tessa announced, her voice husky with sleep. "Happy eighteen-and-one-day, guys."

We crawled out into air that smelled of sun-warmed resin, damp moss, and crushed wild mint along the gravel bar. Down at the creek bend, thin ribbons of silver mist were rising off the deep emerald pool where sixty-degree morning sun met forty-five-degree Kettle Range snowmelt.

After yesterday's five-mile haul uphill in June heat and sleeping in our clothes, all three of us were sticky with pine pitch, trail dust, and sweat—and in twenty minutes of throwing things into backpacks yesterday afternoon, nobody had thought to pack a swimsuit. Even if we had, soaking our only pairs of denim jeans or cotton shirts in cold mountain water without a clothesline day ahead would leave us shivering by dusk.

None of us hesitated or made a production out of it. Having shared the same womb, the same nursery, and every cramped space of our lives, there had never been an ounce of awkwardness or self-consciousness between the three of us. Out on the wide, sun-baked slab of speckled gray granite overlooking the deep pool, we tugged off our boots and stacked our dry clothes in one neat, folded pile well clear of any splash—my jeans and faded olive flannel layered flat with Tessa's cream henley and Sadie's striped tee on top so the morning sun would warm the cotton while we washed.

"On three," Tessa dared from the smooth granite lip, toes curling over the edge above four feet of clear, dark-green water. "No chickening out halfway down the shin."

"One, two—go!" Sadie called, and all three of us jumped in together.

The shock of pure Kettle Crest snowmelt knocked the wind clean out of my lungs in a giant, echoing yelp as we surfaced in a burst of white spray, gasping and laughing hard enough to startle a kingfisher off an overhanging cedar bough. Beneath the dark-emerald mirror of the surface, clean gravel dropped to chest depth where the current swirled slow and gentle behind the boulder eddy. Tessa and Sadie immediately splashed two strokes inward from either side and latched onto me for body heat, laughing through chattering teeth as we huddled shoulder to shoulder with cold ripples lapping at our collarbones.

"Okay, that wakes you right up!" Tessa gasped, dunking the crown of her chestnut hair backward to rinse the trail dust clean before scrubbing pine pitch off her forearms with wet sand from the shallow shelf.

"Cleanest water in the state of Washington, though," Sadie laughed, slicking her wet hair back behind her ears and leaning warm against my left side while the sun slanted bright through the tall firs onto our faces.

Five minutes in that icy pool washed every last trace of our parents' house off our skin. We scrambled back up onto the sun-warmed granite ledge—skin tingling pink and electric in the warm mountain breeze—stretched out side by side on the hot flat rock wrapped in our one spare wool camp blanket, and huddled close together in a tight three-way tangle until the June sun dried our hair and warmed us straight to the bone before pulling our sun-toasted shirts and dry jeans back on.

***

#### 10:30 AM — Naming the Creek

Over hot oatmeal simmered with a handful of sweet wild strawberries Tessa had spotted along the sunny edge of the clearing, Sadie spread her laminated Forest Service topographic quad flat across the cedar log and weighted its corners down with four smooth creek pebbles.

"Here's the washed-out culvert down on Spur 470-B," Sadie said, tracing a fingernail up the tight brown contour lines into a wide, unnumbered drainage crease tucked between two timbered granite spurs. "Look at the map. The main creek three miles west has a name, and the ridge north of us has an elevation marker, but this whole side fork doesn't even have a label."

I glanced upstream where two towering western red cedars leaned their feathery green boughs across the granite cascade, dropping reddish-gold bark ribbons into the clear foam.

"Cedar Run," I said.

Tessa paused with her tin spoon halfway to her mouth, looked up at the two giant cedars framing the falls, and nodded once, decisive. "Cedar Run. That's it. That's its name."

Sadie uncapped her pencil, wrote *Cedar Run* lightly in the margin of the map laminate, and then looked across the log at Tessa and me. Her voice turned quiet and steady—the voice she always used right before saying out loud what all three of us were already thinking.

"Dad thinks two nights on the hard ground with cold beans is going to break us," Sadie said. "He told Mom before we even reached the fence that we'd be back on the porch steps by Friday morning agreeing to split up."

Tessa's jaw set. She looked down at the braided brown leather cord around her wrist, then back up at the two of us. "I would rather sleep under spruce boughs in the rain until Christmas than walk back through that door and let them put three hundred miles between us."

I reached down beside my boot and unrolled Grandpa Owen's heavy waxed-canvas tool wrap across my knees. Inside their oiled pockets gleamed the tools Owen Sawyer had taught me to sharpen on Sunday afternoons when I was fifteen: a two-pound hickory camp hatchet, a curved two-handled drawknife, an L-shaped steel shingle froe for splitting cedar bolts along the grain, a twenty-four-inch Swedish bow-saw blade coiled neatly inside a strip of split garden hose, two wood chisels, a hand auger bit, a Dual-Grit whetstone, a fifty-foot hank of braided nylon cord, and an altoids-sized tin of trout hooks, lead split-shot, and monofilament line.

"Look around us," I said quietly, resting my palm over the cool steel of Grandpa Owen's froe. "We aren't going back down that hill, and we don't need a town right now either. Nobody knows we're past the washout. We have clean spring water, deadfall red cedar that splits straight as lumber, wild berries coming ripe, and trout rising in every pool down this creek. If we use our heads and start gathering food and building a real shelter right now while June is warm, we can make a camp out here that holds all three of us."

Tessa's eyes lit up with that fierce, kinetic spark she always got the second a problem turned into something she could build with her hands.

"Not just a nylon tent," Tessa said, leaning forward with both elbows on her knees. "Something off the ground. Something dry when the mountain storms roll through—something we can keep adding onto week by week."

"First rule Grandpa Owen always taught," Sadie agreed, tapping the contour lines around our nameless valley. "Before you drive the first peg, walk the whole drainage. Let's see what food is growing within a mile of this water and find the single best piece of ground in the valley to build on."

## Chapter 2: Three Footholds in the Valley

### Food, Timber, and the Fork in the Drainage

**Date:** 2000-06-22 at 09:15 America/Los_Angeles

![Luke, Tessa, and Sadie Bridger standing together on a sunlit granite pine overlook in Colville National Forest scouting the wild Cedar Run valley below—surveying the creek-bend red-cedar bench, the sunlit granite cliff overhang above a berry meadow, and the high tamarack saddle.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/cedar-run/e001/jun_2000_cedar_run_scouting_three_footholds.jpg)

#### 9:15 AM — Four Days of Pantry Oats

On our second full morning in the woods, Tessa spread a clean cotton bandana flat on the granite hearth-slab and poured out every dry ration left in our three packs so nobody could kid themselves about the math.

Two quart bags of rolled oats. A pound and a half of pinto beans. Three cups of long-grain white rice, half a jar of peanut butter, a small tin of salt, a handful of raisins, and four packets of peppermint tea. Stretched careful, it was four days of full meals—maybe six if we went hungry by noon.

"Good," Tessa said briskly, tying the oat bags shut again before a camp jay perched in the low fir bough could get bold. "Nothing clarifies a morning schedule like seeing the bottom of the oat bag. Every calorie we bring in from the woods today stretches that salt and rice into July."

We left the dome tent staked tight with our sleeping bags aired dry inside, hoisted our food sack twenty feet out on a high tamarack limb well clear of black bears and pine martens, and took one light daypack, the bow saw I had framed that morning out of seasoned hickory saplings, Grandpa Owen's hatchet, and the tin of trout hooks.

Our mission for the day was simple: scout the entire Cedar Run basin—bottomland, sunny south slope, and upper springhead—before driving a single permanent stake.

We didn't have to walk a hundred yards from our Night One fire ring to examine our first contender.

Just above our gravel bar, where Cedar Run slowed through three linked pools of deep green water, a monarch western red cedar nearly five feet thick at the butt had blown down in some winter gale ten or fifteen years back. Its roots had tipped up clean out of the slope, leaving eighty straight, knot-free feet of rot-proof red heartwood resting dry off the damp soil along a level pine-needle terrace twenty feet above the high-water mark. Down in the emerald pool below the root wad, three spotted cutthroat trout—ten to twelve inches long, bellies flashing pale gold—hung steady in the current sipping caddisflies off the surface film.

I tapped the side of the fallen cedar trunk with the poll of Grandpa Owen's hatchet. Beneath an inch of gray weathered sapwood, the heartwood rang solid as a church bell and smelled sweet and peppery.

"Do you realize how many roof shakes and wall planks are sitting inside this one tree?" I said, running my palm along the laser-straight grain. "We wouldn't have to fell a single green tree. With the froe and a hardwood maul, we could split raised floorboards, frame a whole three-sided lean-to right against the trunk as a windbreak, and check willow fish traps in those three pools morning and night."

"And look along the gravel bar," Tessa added, crouching beside a dense thicket of flexible red-osier dogwood shoots and broad-leafed thimbleberry canes loaded with white blossoms turning to green fruit. "Straight basket shoots for fish traps right at the water's edge, wild mint, stinging nettle tops we can boil like spinach tonight, and cold water twenty steps from the hearth."

"Wait till we check the higher ground during rain runoff, though," Sadie pointed out, squinting upslope where bright sunlight blazed across an open bench half a mile north. "Creek bottoms stay ten degrees colder once September frost sinks into the canyon. Let's climb the south face."

***

#### 1:20 PM — The Granite Overhang and the High Springhouse

Climbing out of the cool cedar shade onto the south-facing slope felt like stepping into another season altogether.

Here the dark timber gave way to five rolling acres of open mountain meadow warm with late-June sunshine and buzzing with wild bumblebees. Knee-high mountain huckleberry bushes carpeted the slope in thick green waves, their undersides already heavy with thousands of swelling berries blush-pink and turning dusty blue in the sunniest pockets, mixed with wild onions whose green tops smelled sharp and savory the instant Tessa bruised one between her thumb and forefinger.

At the top of that sunny meadow rose a forty-foot rib of pale gray Kettle Range granite—and carved into its base by ancient ice sat a natural rock overhang twelve feet deep, twenty-five feet wide, and taller than my head.

I stepped under the stone lip out of the hot noon sun and dropped to one knee to feel the floor. Bone-dry granite dust and papery pine needles that hadn't seen a single drop of rain in a hundred winters. Better still, the solid rock ceiling sloped upward toward a natural vertical cleft at the eastern corner like a ready-made chimney flue, and the massive cliff face soaked up direct southern sunshine from nine in the morning until seven at night.

"Feel the rock wall," Tessa said, pressing both palms flat against the back of the overhang. "It's like laying your hands on a warm bread oven. If we dry-stacked flat granite slabs and peeled lodgepole poles across just half of this front opening and chinked the gaps with creek clay and moss, three of our four walls are solid mountain bedrock—and all those huckleberries and wild onions are right outside our front door."

"Water's a quarter-mile downhill to the creek," I noted, measuring the haul in my head, "unless that dampfern seam at the east end of the meadow seeps into a catch-basin."

"Let's check the upper springhead on the map before we call it," Sadie said, pointing up the narrow timbered draw to the northeast where a ribbon of bright green tamaracks climbed toward a high saddle.

An hour's steady climb along a mossy trickling run brought us out onto a level, wind-sheltered saddle framed by tall golden-barked Douglas firs and feathery larches—and stopped all three of us dead in our tracks in the middle of a patch of wild red raspberries.

Half-buried beneath sixty years of emerald feather-moss and creeping twinflower stood four waist-to-shoulder-high walls of hand-fitted gray fieldstone, twelve feet by fourteen, laid straight and plumb by some forgotten nineteen-thirties forest crew long before our parents were even born. The old cedar roof poles had rotted away decades ago, letting sunshine pour straight into the stone enclosure, save for one low corner arch where crystal-clear forty-two-degree spring water bubbled straight up out of clean gravel into a hand-chiseled stone trough before spilling merrily down the draw toward Cedar Run.

"No way," Tessa whispered, stepping through the old stone doorway and dipping two fingers into the icy trough. "A stone springhouse! Look at the walls—there isn't a loose rock in the whole foundation, and we've got cold running water and a natural refrigerator right inside the stonework!"

Sadie walked the outside perimeter, parting lush fronds of lady-fern where golden chanterelle buttons were already pushing through damp fir duff after last week's rain, while two plump dusky grouse burst clucking out of the serviceberry brush thirty yards up the game trail.

"Nobody has walked up this draw in forty years," Sadie marveled, resting her hand on the warm, moss-velveted corner stone. "New Douglas fir ridgepole, peeled rafters, overlapping red-cedar bark shingles overhead—we'd have four stone walls and clean water under one roof."

***

#### 6:40 PM — Three Visions Around the Keyhole Fire

Back down at our dome tent as golden evening slanted low through the ponderosas, we boiled a pot of tender young nettle greens with a half-cup of rice and a pinch of salt—our very first half-foraged supper—and sat close together on our cedar log while Grandpa Owen's guitar rested across Sadie's lap.

All three places we had found today stayed 100% inside the deep forest, miles from any road or town, and any one of them could keep the three of us fed and dry if we poured our backs into it starting at dawn tomorrow. Yet each foothold meant committing to a completely different way of living and building.

Down here on the Creek-Bend Cedar Bench, we had that eighty-foot fallen red-cedar giant waiting to split into straight boards and roof shakes alongside endless cutthroat trout in the deep pools right below our boots. Up on the South-Facing Granite Overhang, three indestructible walls of sun-warmed bedrock stood bone-dry above five acres of ripening huckleberries, wild onions, and meadow roots. And high on the saddle at the Forgotten Stone Springhouse, four nineteen-thirties fieldstone walls and a cold bubbling spring trough waited for new rafters beside rich mushroom duff and grouse trails.

Tessa leaned against my right shoulder; Sadie rested her knee against my left, fingers quiet on the guitar strings, both of my sisters looking at me in the warm amber glow as the creek murmured past the stones.

"Tomorrow morning we start building for real, and we start laying up food," Sadie said softly. "Where do we stake our claim, Luke?"

# Route 1: The Creek-Bend Cedar Bench

## Chapter 3: Straight Grain and Cutthroat Pools

### Splitting the Windfall Giant

**Date:** 2000-06-23 at 07:30 America/Los_Angeles

![Beside a deep emerald creek pool in Colville National Forest, Luke Bridger splits straight western red cedar planks from a massive fallen trunk using a steel froe and wooden maul while Tessa sets a hand-woven willow trout trap in the creek eddy and Sadie stacks flat river hearth stones.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/cedar-run/paths/jun_2000_route1_creek_bend_splitting_cedar_shakes.jpg)

#### 7:30 AM — The Sound of Straight Heartwood

The steel blade of Grandpa Owen's shingle froe bit into the end-grain of the thirty-inch western red cedar round, and when I struck it twice with the heavy tamarack maul I had carved before breakfast, the bolt popped open down its center with a clean, sweet *crack* that smelled like a cedar chest opened after fifty years.

"Look at that split!" Sadie called from six paces away, setting down two flat gray granite hearth slabs to run her fingertips along the freshly riven plank. Not a twist, not a knot—thirty inches long, eight inches wide, and flat enough to lay a carpenter's level across.

"Grandpa Owen wasn't exaggerating about old-growth windfall," I grinned, wiping sweat off my forehead with the back of my flannel sleeve. "That one fallen trunk is an entire lumberyard sitting twenty steps above the trout pools. It's been seasoning off the ground for ten summers and there isn't a spot of rot an inch inside the bark."

Choosing the creek-bend bench meant our mornings began to the music of rushing water and clean wood splitting in the cool canyon shade. Before we did anything else, we worked together with the bow saw to buck four stout cedar rounds off the narrower crown end of the windfall giant and rolled them into the four corners of a ten-by-eight-foot rectangle right alongside the five-foot main trunk—using the massive fallen tree itself as our solid, draft-proof north back wall.

While I notched stout peeled Douglas fir stringers across those four corner blocks to raise our sleeping deck eighteen inches clear of damp ground and evening cold air, Tessa knelt down on the sunlit gravel bar with an armful of whip-straight red-osier dogwood and willow shoots she had harvested at dawn.

Watching Tessa's hands work was like watching someone weave music out of green switches. Using thinner peeled cedar-root lashings so we wouldn't waste a single foot of our nylon cord, she wove an elongated, funnel-throated basket trap three feet long—wide cone at the upstream mouth tapering to a narrow inward-pointing throat inside a tied tail-cap—the exact passive creek weir we had once seen sketched in an old pioneer woodcraft book on Grandpa Owen's shelf.

***

#### 1:45 PM — Silver-Gold in the Willow Trap

By early afternoon, twelve wide split-cedar planks lay lashed snug and level across our raised fir stringers—a solid, sweet-smelling wooden floor wide enough for all three of us to stretch out side by side without a single rock or root poking through—and six peeled fir rafters sloped upward from the giant cedar trunk to a sturdy front ridgepole supported on forked uprights, waiting for our overlapping cedar roof shakes.

Down at the middle emerald pool, Tessa let out a low, triumphant whistle.

Sadie and I scrambled down the granite bank right as Tessa waded thigh-deep in her rolled-up jeans to lift the willow funnel trap out of the narrow stone chute between two boulders, water cascading silver through the woven red-osier ribs. Inside the basket flashed three fat, cold-firm cutthroat trout—ten to twelve inches apiece, olive backs speckled dark and crimson slashes bright beneath their jaws—fighting the wet willow weave.

"Supper just swam into the dining room!" Tessa beamed, cradling the basket ashore onto the dry gravel bar.

She kept the two largest twelve-inchers—dispatching them swiftly and cleanly before gutting them on a flat wet stone at the tail of the riffle so the cold current carried every trace downstream away from camp—and slipped the smaller ten-incher gently back into the pool to grow for August. Up on the sunny edge of the clearing, Sadie had filled our enamel kettle half-full of tiny, intensely sweet wild mountain strawberries and tender fireweed shoots.

That evening, as blue canyon shadow climbed the eastern pines while high golden sun still gilded the treetops overhead, we christened our new raised cedar deck and our U-shaped river-stone reflector hearth.

Tessa skewered the two split cutthroat fillets flat on green peeled alder sticks leaned over a bed of glowing pine-and-alder coals until the skin blistered crisp and golden and fat sizzled into the embers. A pinch of our precious salt turned fresh snowmelt trout, boiled fireweed greens, and a shared cup of wild strawberries into the richest feast any of the three of us had ever tasted—and we hadn't touched a single grain of our dry oats or pinto beans all day.

After we scrubbed our fingers with clean creek sand and banked fresh wood against the stone reflector wall so warmth radiated straight back onto our raised cedar platform, Sadie leaned back against the giant fallen trunk between Tessa and me and lifted Grandpa Owen's guitar.

With a solid cedar floor beneath us, two dozen freshly split roof shakes stacked ready to overlap across the rafters tomorrow morning, and the three deep pools murmuring twenty feet below our toes, Sadie thumb-picked a bright, rippling chord progression that sounded exactly like sunlight dancing over creek stones—and when Tessa and I joined our voices to hers on the chorus, the giant red cedar behind our backs hummed right along with the strings.

***

# Route 2: The South-Facing Granite Overhang

## Chapter 3: Living Stone and Huckleberry Sun

### Three Walls Already Standing

**Date:** 2000-06-23 at 07:45 America/Los_Angeles

![Beneath a wide, sun-warmed south-facing granite cliff overhang overlooking a lush mountain huckleberry meadow in Colville National Forest, Luke, Tessa, and Sadie Bridger stack flat granite fieldstones and peeled lodgepole pine poles to enclose their rock shelter.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/cedar-run/paths/jun_2000_route2_granite_overhang_stone_wall_huckleberries.jpg)

#### 7:45 AM — Moving Upslope Into the Sun

By eight in the morning, while cool blue shadow still lingered down in the narrow creek bottom, our south-facing granite overhang half a mile upslope was already bathed in warm gold sunlight.

We struck the blue-and-white dome tent in ten minutes, packed every pot, sleeping bag, and tool up the deer switchbacks in a single trip, and set Grandpa Owen's black guitar case deep in the dry, sheltered curve at the back of the stone alcove where rain hadn't touched the dust since the glaciers melted.

Standing inside that twelve-foot-deep pocket of solid Kettle Range bedrock felt like discovering the earth itself had already done three-quarters of our heavy lifting. Overhead, a thick shelf of pale gray granite projected straight out like a fortress porch roof; behind us and along both curving sides, seamless living rock blocked every gust of wind off the northern ridge; and at the eastern corner, a natural V-shaped vertical fissure rose fifteen feet to the cliff top like a stonemason's chimney flue.

Best of all, Sadie had been right about the bright green fern seep sixty yards east across the meadow edge. When Tessa dug two feet down into the cold gravel at the base of a mossy granite outcrop with a flat digging stick and lined the basin with clean cobbles, icy groundwater cleared within twenty minutes into a brimming, crystal-pure dipping spring right on our own contour line.

"Water sixty steps east, five acres of berries out the front door, and a roof ten thousand tons heavy," Tessa grinned, wiping damp granite grit off her wrists onto her jeans. "Let's give this place a front wall."

All morning and well past noon, the three of us worked an assembly line across the talus scatter at the base of the cliff. Centuries of frost-wedging had left hundreds of flat, brick-like granite slabs lying among the pinegrass. Sadie and Tessa carried stones two-handed on a sturdy canvas sling while I fitted them dry-stack style—wide base courses two feet thick, overlapping every vertical seam two-on-one and wedging small shim stones tight until not a single block rocked under my boot heel—raising a chest-high stone wind-wall across two-thirds of the overhang's open mouth while leaving a sheltered south doorway facing the sunny meadow.

Above the stone half-wall, we wedged dead-standing peeled lodgepole pine poles upright into natural ceiling crevices and packed the chinks between the stones with stiff gray clay dug from the spring seep kneaded thick with dry pine needles and moss.

***

#### 4:30 PM — Purple Fingers and Stored Heat

When our forearms finally shook too hard to lift one more forty-pound slab of granite, we grabbed our clean kettle, two empty zip-top bags, and Sadie's spare bandana and waded straight out into the sunlit ocean of mountain huckleberry brush below our doorway.

In the hottest south-facing pockets along the warm boulders, the early bushes were already loaded with plump, dark-purple berries the size of marbles. For two solid hours the three of us moved side by side down the rows, fingers staining deep violet-blue, eating one berry for every four that plinked into the kettle while Tessa also pried up two dozen crisp white wild-onion bulbs from the friable meadow loam with a fire-hardened digging stick.

On a wide, table-flat slab of sun-hot granite just outside our stone doorway, Sadie spread half of our berry harvest in a single thin layer to begin sun-drying into chewy winter trail fruit—covering them lightly with cheesecloth-thin pine twigs to keep the camp jays honest—while we carried the rest inside to our newly laid corner stone hearth beneath the natural rock chimney cleft.

When I touched a spark to dry pine kindling under that eastern cleft, we held our breath for five seconds to test the draft.

The curl of pale woodsmoke didn't roll back into our eyes; warmed by the sun-heated cliff face, it pulled straight up the vertical granite fissure like a chimney pipe and vanished cleanly into the open sky twenty feet overhead.

We simmered a thick, savory pot of wild-onion rice alongside warm stewed huckleberries, then spread a deep, fragrant mattress of dry western larch boughs and fern fronds beneath our sleeping bags against the curved rear rock wall.

An hour after sundown, when the open meadow air dropped twenty degrees into crisp mountain night, the magic of that south-facing cliff revealed itself. Every square foot of granite behind our backs and above our heads had soaked up ten hours of blazing June sunshine—and now it breathed gentle, steady radiant warmth back into our enclosed alcove like a tiled masonry stove.

Curled up side by side in our shirt-sleeves with Tessa tucked warm against one side of me and Sadie fingerpicking a slow, glowing nineteen-seventy-five meadow ballad on the other, watching tiny orange embers drift up the stone cleft while our rock walls held us warm, we knew we had found a fortress.

***

# Route 3: The Forgotten Stone Springhouse

## Chapter 3: Cold Spring and Tamarack Rafters

### Sixty Years Under the Moss

**Date:** 2000-06-23 at 08:00 America/Los_Angeles

![In a sunlit high mountain tamarack clearing in Colville National Forest, Luke and Tessa Bridger hoist peeled Douglas fir roof rafters onto the moss-covered dry-stone walls of a forgotten 1930s springhouse while Sadie hands up wide strips of western red cedar bark.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/cedar-run/paths/jun_2000_route3_forgotten_stone_springhouse_rafters.jpg)

#### 8:00 AM — Reclaiming the Four Walls

Whoever had laid the four fieldstone walls on the high tamarack saddle back in the nineteen-thirties had built as if they expected the mountain itself to lean on their work.

Once Tessa, Sadie, and I cleared out the wrist-thick alder saplings, rotted old roof humus, and sixty years of fallen cones from the twelve-by-fourteen-foot interior, we hit a floor of flat, fitted gray slate flags swept clean along the northern wall by the forty-two-degree spring bubbling endlessly through its chiseled stone trough. Not a single corner course had bowed or slumped; the walls stood a solid five feet high on the side slopes and rose to seven-foot stone gables at either end, complete with a square stone lintel over the narrow south doorway.

"Somebody loved this spot once," Sadie said quietly, brushing emerald moss gently off a hand-dressed doorway block without disturbing the tiny white twinflowers rooted in the outer crevices. "Look how dry the upper nine-tenths of the flagstone floor stays—the spring trough has its own stone lip and outlet channel straight under the east wall."

"Which means drinking water, wash water, and an icebox that never warms above forty-two degrees without taking one step outside in a storm," Tessa said, testing the gable notch at the peak of the west wall with both hands. "Luke, if we drop one straight sixteen-foot Douglas fir ridgepole right into these original gable notches, we can have rafters up before supper."

High on the breezy saddle, fallen dead-standing timber cured hard and bone-dry instead of rotting into sponge. Halfway across the larch bench we found three arrow-straight, wind-snapped Douglas fir poles seasoned silver-gray and light enough for the three of us to shoulder together—Luke at the heavy butt end, Tessa and Sadie paired at the tip—marching in step through the wild raspberry canes like a three-person barn-raising crew.

Heaving that sixteen-foot ridgepole up onto the seven-foot stone gables took every ounce of leverage we had, walking it up forked push-poles inch by inch on Sadie's steady three-count until the timber dropped home into both stone pockets with a deep, satisfying *thud* that shook dust off the moss.

All afternoon, while I notched twelve peeled tamarack rafters to straddle the ridgepole down to the side walls and lashed them fast with green spruce-root withes, Tessa and Sadie peeled four-foot sheets of thick, fibrous bark off two long-dead fallen cedars down in the draw—overlapping them shingle-fashion from eaves to ridge and weighting them down with heavy pole binders so even a mountain downburst would shed clean past the stonework.

***

#### 5:50 PM — Golden Chanterelles and the High Saddle View

With half of our roof already shingled tight over the dry western sleeping bay—and our blue-and-white tent fly stretched taut across the remaining half until tomorrow's bark haul—Tessa and Sadie disappeared into the damp, shadowy Douglas fir duff along the north rim for forty-five minutes with our clean bandana and Grandpa Owen's pocketknife.

They came back grinning ear to ear with nearly three pounds of bright, apricot-gold Pacific golden chanterelles—trumpet-shaped ridges running cleanly down firm white stems, smelling faintly of ripe apricots and damp earth—along with a quart of tart red wild raspberries and two handfuls of lemony wood sorrel leaves.

"Look at the cold trough work," Sadie said proudly, submerging our sealed jar of peanut butter and a covered tin of extra chanterelles two inches deep in the crystal stone spring channel along the north wall, where cold water purled steadily around the metal like a mountain dairy house.

Outside the south doorway on a natural stone terrace that looked out across twenty miles of rolling green Kettle River ridges—not a road, rooftop, or power line in sight, only wave after wave of pine and larch turning copper in the low sun—we built a compact stone cooking hearth and sautéed sliced golden chanterelles in a spoonful of peanut-butter oil with wood sorrel and a cup of boiled rice.

Nothing in any restaurant on earth could have touched hot golden mushrooms and cold spring water on that ridge at eight o'clock on a June evening.

When twilight cooled the high saddle, we carried a flat stone pan of glowing hardwood coals inside to warm the slate flags, pulled a rough-woven cedar-bough curtain across the low doorway, and stretched our sleeping bags side by side under the fresh-peeled tamarack rafters in the dry bay. Listening to the quiet, musical trickle of our indoor stone spring in the dark while Sadie hummed a soft harmony line between Tessa and me, it felt less like camping and more like coming home to a place that had been waiting sixty years for the three of us to put its roof back on.

***
