# Book One: Beyond the Studio Rift

## Chapter 1: Two Names on One Studio Lease

### Part 1: Route 13, Forty-Eight Hours' Notice, and Home to Emerald Creek

**Harmonic Cycle:** Autumn 2000 — Two Days Before Gwen's Eighteenth Birthday, Earth • **Chronometer:** 08:30

![On the cozy covered front porch of Grandpa Walter's brick bungalow near Emerald Creek Mall in Chesapeake on an overcast October afternoon, taller seventeen-year-old Gwen Lennox (6-foot-1, in a modest burgundy plaid flannel shirt and jeans) wraps both arms joyfully and protectively around her shorter twenty-three-year-old brother Nolan Lennox (5-foot-7, in a navy tee and olive jacket with his battered duffel bag at his feet) after he breaks free from his abusive girlfriend at the Norfolk bus station, while all four of their matte-silver and matte-charcoal single-tube Lofstrand forearm crutches lean cleanly against the wooden porch railing beside them.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/e001/e001_c001_p001_safe_home_emerald_creek_porch.jpg)

#### 8:30 AM — Petty Fights, Day-Of Whims, and Keeping My Plan to Myself

By eight-thirty on Tuesday morning inside Tammy's smoke-stained single-wide trailer at Pinecrest Court, we had already fought twice before coffee—once because my manual wheelchair tire left a damp mark two inches inside the linoleum doorway, and ten minutes later because I asked where my clean navy t-shirts had gone.

For weeks now, every single day with Tammy had been a minefield of petty, exhausting arguments over nothing at all: which shelf of the fridge my work lunch touched, how loud my Lofstrand forearm crutches clicked when I stood up at night, how many minutes I spent on the corded wall phone checking on seventeen-year-old Gwen and dying Grandpa Walter over near Emerald Creek Mall. Nine months after letting her talk me into moving out of Grandpa's house, the fog had finally burned off inside my twenty-three-year-old head. I was completely done. I had already decided I was breaking up with her—the only question was how to get myself, my wheelchair, and my crutches safely out of an isolated trailer park off Route 13 without Tammy blocking the narrow aluminum doorway or smashing my things in a rage.

Then, at quarter past eight that very morning, Tammy had slammed her bedroom closet open, hauled out an overstuffed rolling suitcase, and announced on pure day-of impulse that we were catching the city bus to the Norfolk Pathfinder station right now to buy two ninety-nine-dollar walk-up cross-country tickets to Seattle, Washington.

Sitting quietly in my manual wheelchair with my battered olive duffel across my thighs and both of my matte-silver Lofstrand forearm crutches strapped snug in the vertical holder tubes on the back of my backrest, I hadn't argued a single word.

Inside my own head, two thoughts clicked into place with crystal clarity.

First, I had zero intention of setting foot on any Pathfinder bus. Gwen was turning eighteen in two days, Grandpa Walter's heart was down to its final borrowed days, and wild horses couldn't drag me out of Chesapeake.

Second, anybody who actually used a wheelchair in the year two thousand knew Pathfinder's intercity accessibility rule backward and forward: if you needed an over-the-road coach equipped with a hydraulic wheelchair lift—not just leaving Norfolk, either, across every transfer hub from Richmond and Roanoke down Interstate 81 to Knoxville, Nashville, St. Louis, Kansas, Denver, Wyoming, Idaho, and Pasco—you had to call Pathfinder at least forty-eight hours in advance so dispatch could assign lift-equipped coaches along the route. Deciding on a three-thousand-mile trip the morning of departure without giving two seconds of thought to whether her boyfriend in a wheelchair could physically board the bus was Tammy in a nutshell. I was ninety-nine percent sure she had never called, and I sure hadn't reminded her or dialed their number myself—because I already knew I wasn't going.

Even better, letting her march me out to the bus stop meant getting my wheelchair, my crutches, and a duffel bag of my clothes out of Pinecrest Court in broad daylight, straight into a public transit terminal full of witnesses and city buses heading back toward Emerald Creek.

This was my chance to escape.

"Quit sitting there daydreaming and roll out on the porch so I can lock the deadbolt," Tammy snapped, shoving an envelope of crumpled twenties into her purse.

Out on the rutted gravel lane past sagging trailer skirting, weeds, and overflowing dumpsters—easy rolling distance from where the buses met up along Robert Hall Boulevard—getting into Downtown Norfolk meant catching CVTA Route 6 or Route 13, whichever showed up first. When we reached the cracked curb along the Route 13 corridor, a forty-foot high-floor Beacon V coach wearing old nineties white, navy, and teal Tidewater stripes with a rectangular white CVTA decal beside the front doors rumbled up first on Route 13.

The operator pulled the air brake, flipped a dashboard toggle so the three steep front stairwell steps flattened into the horizontal steel platform of the Elevate-U hydraulic wheelchair lift, and lowered it smoothly to the concrete. Tammy let out an exaggerated, put-upon sigh right behind my push-handles the entire thirty seconds it took the hydraulic arms to raise my wheelchair three feet up to the cabin floor and lock the floor straps in place.

"Thirteen-oh-two to Dispatch," the operator called into his handset as Tammy dropped coins impatiently into the farebox.

<pa>Dispatch, go ahead thirteen-oh-two.</pa>

"Dispatch, I've picked up one ten-sixteen on Route 13 inbound."

<pa>Ten-four.</pa>

Tammy marched halfway down the aisle by herself so she wouldn't have to sit beside the wheelchair bay. Watching scrap yards and river bridges roll past the window with my silver forearm crutches rattling softly in the holder tubes behind my shoulders, my pulse beat fast and steady against my ribs. Just get to Downtown Norfolk. Get inside the terminal doors. End it in public where she can't lock me in.

***

#### 9:45 AM — Before the Walk-Up Ticket Counter

Near the concrete dome of the Monticello Arena in Downtown Norfolk, our Route 13 operator cycled the front-stairwell Elevate-U lift back down to the sidewalk. Crossing over to the northbound stop to catch CVTA Route 2 or Route 11—whichever showed up first—for the short hop up to the intercity station, a high-floor Route 11 MetroCoach beat a trailing Route 2 coach through the traffic signal by half a block, lifted my chair aboard, and set us down five minutes later a block from the brutalist concrete facade of the Norfolk Pathfinder Terminal.

Heavy automatic sliding glass doors parted onto a wave of industrial floor wax, fryer grease, and diesel exhaust drifting in from the rear coach bays.

Even at quarter to ten on a weekday morning, the waiting room was busy—thirty or forty travelers sitting along bolted rows of hard plastic chairs beneath buzzing fluorescent tubes, two uniformed security guards chatting near the vending machines, and a line of three walk-up customers ahead of the scratched plexiglass ticket counter at the far wall.

Tammy didn't even glance down to check if my caster wheels had cleared the rubber entry mat. Shifting her rolling suitcase into her left hand and pulling the cash envelope halfway out of her purse with her right, she started striding across the middle of the waiting room straight toward the walk-up ticket line.

"Wait right here by the pillar while I go buy our Seattle tickets," Tammy tossed back over her shoulder.

Before she could take two steps toward that ticket counter, I snapped both steel wheel locks down hard on my manual wheelchair tires right in the middle of the public concourse.

"Save your money, Tammy," I said clearly.

She stopped mid-stride and turned halfway around, eyebrows pinching together in annoyance. "What?"

My heart hammered against my breastbone, yet looking up from my wheelchair with thirty witnesses around us instead of four thin trailer walls, the words came out steady and final.

"Don't buy a ticket for me," I said. "First off, you decided on this trip two hours ago, and you have to call Pathfinder forty-eight hours in advance just to get a wheelchair lift on an intercity bus—which you didn't even bother to think about. Second, Gwen turns eighteen in two days and my grandfather is dying. I'm not going across the country with you, I'm not coming back to the trailer, and we're breaking up right here."

For three dead-silent seconds Tammy stared at me with her hand frozen on her purse envelope, realizing all at once that I had let her ride two city buses into Norfolk just to walk out on her in public. Every petty argument of the past two months detonated behind her eyes into ugly, blotchy red fury.

"You sneaky little bastard!" Tammy shrieked, dropping her suitcase handle so loud heads whipped around in every row of seats as she lunged three fast steps straight back to the front of my wheelchair. "You let me haul my bags all the way into Norfolk just to dump me in a bus station?! Nobody cares about your forty-eight-hour cripple excuses or your six-foot freak of a sister—you're coming up to that counter with me right now!"

"No, Tammy," I said, gripping both wheel rims tight while my throat knotted up. "Go wherever you want. We're done."

Tammy didn't say another word. Towering straight over my seated frame in the middle of the public waiting area, she swung her right arm back and violently smacked me flat across the left side of my face with all her weight.

The crack rang off the tile walls like a board snapping in half.

My head jerked hard against my right shoulder, knocking my neck stiff while blinding white heat bloomed across my cheek and ear. Sitting locked in my manual wheelchair with nowhere to back up, nine straight months of walking on eggshells, petty daily cruelty, being cut off from Gwen and Grandpa Walter, and suddenly getting struck across the face in front of an entire bus terminal broke the dam inside my chest completely apart.

My breath hitched in a ragged, broken gasp, and I started crying right there in my wheelchair in the middle of the waiting room—heavy, chest-shaking sobs pouring out of me before I could stop them, hot tears spilling down my stinging face onto my jacket collar while I pressed both palms over my eyes.

Tammy raised her hand again, teeth bared over my chair. "Stop crying like a pathetic little—"

Fellow passengers moved faster than she could swing.

Plastic chairs scraped loud across the terrazzo as a broad-shouldered shipyard worker in a navy canvas coat lunged straight between Tammy and my footrests, catching her forearm and shoving her firmly backward toward her dropped suitcase while a middle-aged woman in teal hospital scrubs and a young guy in a culinary jacket stepped in shoulder-to-shoulder around my wheelchair.

"Get back off him right now—don't you dare touch him again!" the shipyard worker barked, pushing Tammy back another full pace when she tried to claw past his chest while two more passengers yelled for the uniformed guards by the vending bank. "Security! Right here—this woman just hauled off and smacked a man sitting in a wheelchair!"

"Stay out of my business!" Tammy screamed, face purple, clutching her unspent cash envelope as the two security officers jogged up fast to flank her elbows.

"Out the front doors right now, ma'am, or Norfolk PD puts cuffs on you," the older guard ordered flatly while the culinary student rolled her suitcase out behind her heels so she couldn't step back within ten feet of my chair.

My whole chest kept heaving in jagged, humiliating sobs I couldn't turn off. Kneeling down to eye level beside my right wheel so she wasn't towering over me, the woman in teal scrubs pressed a clean stack of folded paper napkins gently into my hand and spoke in the kindest, steadying voice imaginable.

"You're completely safe now, sweetheart—she's out on the sidewalk with security and she can't get back in," she said softly. "Just breathe. Where's home for you? Have you got family waiting?"

Wiping furiously at my wet eyes and burning left cheek with the napkins, choking through heavy sobs that broke every sentence in half, I managed to get the words out.

"I just—I just want to go home," I sobbed, gripping my push-rims with shaking hands. "I just want to go home to the Emerald Creek area of Chesapeake... my little sister turns eighteen in two days and my grandpa's dying, and I just want to go home to them."

Nobody looked down on me for crying. An older gray-haired retired chief carrying a canvas tool bag stepped up beside the nurse, made sure the straps holding my silver forearm crutches on the back of my wheelchair hadn't loosened, and nodded calmly toward the side exit away from the front curb.

"Easy three-bus hop gets you straight back to Emerald Creek Mall, son," the retired chief said warmly. "Take the two or the eleven—whichever rolls by first—back down into Downtown Norfolk, catch the twenty out to Glenrock Mall, and hop the fifteen right into Emerald Creek. Come out the side doors with me—I'm heading downtown myself, so I'll ride beside your chair till you're safely on that twenty bus."

***

#### 12:15 PM — Stopping Short of the Grassy Island at Emerald Creek Mall

True to his word, the retired chief waited with me a block off the terminal until a southbound Route 2 Beacon V pulled up ahead of the eleven, rode all the way back into Downtown Norfolk beside my wheelchair, and didn't wave goodbye until the front-stairwell Elevate-U lift on an eastbound CVTA Route 20 coach had raised me safely aboard for the run out Virginia Beach Boulevard.

By the time the salt tracks on my cheeks dried in the overhead air vent and our Route 20 bus approached the wide intersection of Virginia Beach Boulevard and Military Highway just outside Glenrock Mall, the operator unhooked his radio handset to call ahead for my pulse transfer.

"Outbound twenty to the outbound fifteen at Glenrock Mall, I have one ten-sixteen for you," he said into the mic.

<pa>Ten-four. Is he heading towards Emerald Creek Mall?</pa>

The operator glanced up into his wide interior mirror toward my wheelchair bay. "Heading to Emerald Creek Mall?"

"Yes, sir," I managed, my voice still hoarse yet steady.

"Ten-four, he says he's heading to Emerald Creek Mall," the operator confirmed into the handset.

<pa>Ten-four, Route 15 copies.</pa>

At Glenrock Mall, the waiting southbound Route 15 Beacon V held its front doors open while its Elevate-U platform lifted my chair aboard, then rumbled south down all eight lanes of Military Highway—gliding over the low, flat highway bridge across the Eastern Branch of the Elizabeth River and branching off at Old Emerald Creek Road toward the mall.

Anyone who rode a wheelchair to Emerald Creek Mall knew the catch waiting out on Mall Ring Road. Instead of a paved transit shelter or a sidewalk landing pad, CVTA's official Emerald Creek Mall bus stop was nothing more than a metal bus-stop sign and schedule placard driven into the middle of a narrow, oval grassy parking-lot island ringed on all sides by an unbroken six-inch concrete curb—zero pavement and zero curb cuts anywhere on it. If an operator pulled up flush to the sign and lowered the wheelchair lift onto that grassy island, your casters were trapped inside six inches of concrete curbing.

As our forty-foot coach banked onto Mall Ring Road across the sea of parked cars from the department store entrance and eased toward that curbed strip of dry grass, I unlocked my vocal cords and called forward clearly.

"Driver, I'm getting off here at the mall—could you stop short of the island and put me off on the roadway?"

"Sure thing, man—know that curb well," the operator called back with an understanding nod in his mirror. Flipping on his four-way hazard flashers in the wide parking-lot drive lane a full bus length short of the grassy island and the bus-stop post, he pulled the air brake and cycled the front stairwell flat into the Elevate-U platform, lowering my manual wheelchair smoothly onto the level gray asphalt of the parking lot roadway.

Grandpa Walter's house wasn't far from the outer ring road at all. Rolling clear of the mall lot into the quiet maple-lined neighborhood blocks, I locked my wheelchair brakes at the foot of Grandpa's three wooden front steps, unstrapped my matte-silver Lofstrand forearm crutches from the back of my chair, hoisted my olive duffel onto the boards, and climbed up onto the covered front porch just as seventeen-year-old Gwen pushed open the storm door on her tall matte-charcoal crutches.

Six-foot-one Gwen took one look at my red-rimmed hazel eyes, the faint pink mark fading on my left cheek, and my wheelchair sitting parked at the bottom of the steps—and the second I told her hoarsely that I had broken up with Tammy at the Norfolk bus station for good, pure sunshine broke across my little sister's face. Propping both of her charcoal crutches cleanly beside my silver pair against the wooden porch railing, Gwen wrapped both long arms fiercely around my neck and hugged me with all her strength.

"You dumped her—you actually dumped her!" Gwen gasped, laughing through happy tears against my hair on Grandpa's porch. "Oh, Nolan, I missed you so much! You're home for my birthday!"

Inside the warm kitchen, though, seventy-eight-year-old Grandpa Walter sat propped in his worn recliner beneath his dark-green and navy-blue plaid wool blanket, a thick manila accordion folder tied with string resting across his thin knees. His eyes shone proud and deeply relieved to have me back under his roof, yet the shallow rattle in his breathing made it plain he knew his time was almost up—holding on by sheer willpower so Gwen would turn eighteen on Thursday free of any county child-welfare claim.

While Gwen went down the hall to make up my old bed, Grandpa untied the manila folder and quietly walked me through the last of his earthly affairs: his notarized two-page will naming me executor, his veterans discharge and modest burial policy, and the ninety-day payoff statement on the home-equity medical loan he had borrowed five years back to keep our roof patched and wheelchairs rolling—meaning once he passed, the house would have to be sold.

"I'm making Thursday, boy," Grandpa Walter whispered with a faint, steady smile, squeezing my wrist. "I'm seeing your sister turn eighteen. After that, Nolan... make sure she's got a home right beside her brother."

That very afternoon—after a Chesapeake sheriff's deputy stood ten minutes of civil standby outside Pinecrest Court while my Blue Tech coworker loaded my full-sized bed, oak dresser, nineteen-inch television, silver VCR and DVD combo unit, and Quantum OS 2000 Professional laptop into his pickup before Tammy even straggled back from Norfolk—I rolled into a walk-up leasing office four blocks from Emerald Creek Mall and Blue Tech and signed a twelve-month lease on Studio 2B.

It was a tiny rectangular box built for one person. Come Thursday evening over birthday cake, I couldn't wait to hand my sister the second key.

### Part 2: Eighteen Candles, One Brass Key, and Everything Left Behind

**Harmonic Cycle:** Autumn 2000 — Gwen's Eighteenth Birthday and the Weeks After, Earth • **Chronometer:** 18:30

#### 6:30 PM — Eighteen Candles and One Brass Apartment Key

Yellow wax dripped down eighteen crooked birthday candles stuck into a homemade chocolate sheet cake at the center of Grandpa Walter's kitchen table while Gwen beamed bright enough to light the whole Emerald Creek neighborhood by herself.

Grandpa Walter had kept his word. Sitting right at the head of the table in his pressed flannel shirt with his dark-green and navy-blue plaid wool blanket tucked over his knees and his nasal oxygen tubing tucked neatly behind his ears, he clapped slowly along as I finished singing off-key across the laminate tabletop. Gwen—officially eighteen years old since breakfast, six-foot-one even seated tall in her manual wheelchair—blew all eighteen flames out in a single gust before reaching sideways to pull me into a rib-crushing one-armed hug.

"Best birthday since I was ten," Gwen declared, eyes shining as she cut Grandpa the corner piece with extra frosting first. "Grandpa's at the table, my big brother's free of that trailer park off Route 13 for good, and nobody from the county can ever put my name on a minor ward list."

Across the table, Grandpa Walter gave me one small, unmistakable nod.

My palms felt damp against my jeans pocket. For forty-eight hours since signing that walk-up apartment lease four blocks down from Emerald Creek Mall, a quiet knot of nervousness had sat under my ribs—wondering if six-foot-one Gwen would take one look at an eleven-by-eighteen-foot studio box with one bed and wish she had roommates her own age instead. Taking a deep breath, I pulled the fresh brass key on its blue plastic hardware tag out of my pocket and set it down right beside Gwen's cake saucer.

"Okay, it's tiny—literally a rectangular box built for one person, right down the street from my shifts at Blue Tech," I said, rubbing the back of my neck. "Only got my one full-sized bed in the corner and a dorm fridge under the counter. Still... I leased Studio 2B yesterday, Gwenny, and I got a second key cut. Whenever you're ready, do you want to come live with me?"

Gwen stared at the little brass key for half a second—and then let out a joyous gasp that made the forks jump on the placemats.

Instead of hesitating for a split second about square footage, my newly eighteen-year-old sister unlocked her wheelchair brakes, rolled two feet around the table corner, and threw both arms around my neck so exuberantly she nearly tipped me backward off my kitchen chair.

"Are you serious?! Yes! A million times yes!" Gwen laughed, kissing the top of my head before snatching up the brass key like I had just handed her the deed to a castle. "First you finally dump Tammy, and now I actually get to live with my big brother?! Nolan, I don't care if it's the size of a shoebox—we're living together!"

At the head of the table, Grandpa Walter reached both thin hands across the tablecloth to cover Gwen's hand and mine over the brass key. Every ounce of worry that had etched deep lines around his mouth for months seemed to smooth completely away in the warm kitchen light.

"Two grown Lennoxes under one roof," Grandpa whispered, his smile peaceful and whole. "Look after each other now."

#### 7:15 AM, The Next Morning — The Quiet Room and Ten Raised Seals

Grandpa Walter slipped away peacefully in his sleep sometime before six o'clock the next morning, his hands folded neat and calm atop his green-and-navy plaid wool blanket.

Knowing someone is dying prepares you for the grief, maybe ten percent. Nothing on Earth prepares a twenty-three-year-old retail sales associate on forearm crutches for the avalanche of cold, relentless logistics that crashes down on a house the hour someone you love stops breathing.

First came the morning itself: holding tight to Gwen's shaking hand at the living-room window while the visiting hospice nurse listened with her stethoscope, recorded seven-twelve in the morning on a carbon clipboard, called the county non-emergency desk so patrol cars wouldn't run sirens onto Grandpa's quiet street, and waited with us until two soft-spoken men in dark suits arrived from the Chesapeake funeral chapel. Before they wheeled Grandpa out the front door, Gwen gently lifted his dark-green and navy-blue plaid wool blanket off the bedframe and held it folded tight against her chest like armor; she didn't put it down all morning.

By two o'clock that afternoon—burning the first of the measly three paid bereavement shifts Blue Tech corporate policy allowed—I was sitting on a stiff upholstered chair inside a rose-carpeted funeral home arrangement office with my silver forearm crutches propped against my thighs while an arrangement director slid glossy binders across a mahogany desk. Everything had a price tag designed to make exhausted families feel cheap if they blinked: bronze-trimmed caskets that cost more than half a year of my pre-tax Blue Tech register wages, memorial booklets, vault liners. Remembering Grandpa's manila folder word for word, I swallowed my dry throat, slid his modest burial-policy certificate and honorable veterans discharge across the blotter, and held firm on the simple, dignified cremation and graveside military honors Grandpa had written down—plus paying ten dollars apiece for ten official raised-seal copies of the Virginia death certificate.

"Ten copies?" Gwen whispered softly beside me in the hallway afterward, leaning close against my shoulder on her charcoal crutches. "Why so many?"

"Nurse warned me," I murmured tiredly, rubbing my forehead. "Nobody—not the bank, not the court, not the electric company—lets you close a single account without a raised seal."

She wasn't exaggerating. Over the next three weeks, while grieving Grandpa every night and rushing back onto the Blue Tech computer aisles every fourth morning in my royal-blue polo so my paycheck wouldn't bounce Studio 2B's first month's rent, every free daylight hour turned into a mountain of clipboards, long hold queues, and brick courthouse counters.

Down at the Chesapeake Circuit Court clerk's office, I had to stand on my silver crutches at a chest-high marble probate window with Grandpa's original will, swear a formal executor's oath, and open an estate checking account just to pay bills legally. When I called the Social Security office with a death certificate number, a flat voice informed me that because Grandpa had died on the twentieth rather than living through the entire calendar month, the Treasury would automatically claw back his final direct-deposit check straight out of the bank—wiping out seven hundred dollars I had counted on to pay the final electric and natural-gas bills. Then came returning the leased medical oxygen concentrator before another monthly rental fee hit, canceling landline telephone and city water deadlines, filing final medical claim forms, and sitting across Grandpa's worn kitchen table with an estate real-estate agent as she laid out the payoff sheet on Grandpa's home-equity medical loan.

Just like Grandpa had warned me, the bank's ninety-day payoff clock was already ticking. On an hourly electronics-store wage, no mortgage underwriter in Virginia would let me refinance a thirty-eight-thousand-dollar balance. The only house Gwen had ever known had to go up for sale immediately—and forty years of Grandpa Walter's woodworking clamps, dishes, wool coats, bookshelves, and living-room armchairs had to be priced, tagged, and cleared out in a weekend estate sale because none of a three-bedroom bungalow could fit inside an eleven-by-eighteen-foot studio box.

#### 10:40 PM, Twelve Days Later — Not Carrying It Alone

Twelve nights after the funeral, surrounded by taped cardboard boxes and bare picture-hook nails on Grandpa's living-room carpet, I finally hit a wall.

My wrists throbbed from six hours of standing on my silver forearm crutches at Blue Tech followed by four hours of sorting utility shutoff notices and realtor seller disclosures on the floor. Staring at a final hospital billing envelope beside Grandpa's empty recliner spot, my vision blurred hot and sudden. I buried my face in both palms, shoulders hunching tight as that ugly voice from Tammy's trailer whispered in the back of my skull that I was in way over my head.

"Hey—none of that," Gwen's warm voice said instantly.

Before I could even pretend I was just rubbing my eyes, six-foot-one Gwen lowered herself onto the carpet right beside me, wrapped Grandpa Walter's green-and-navy plaid wool blanket across both of our shoulders, and pulled me sideways against her chest in a fierce, steady two-armed hug.

"It's just so much paperwork, Gwenny," I admitted thickly against her collarbone, ashamed my voice cracked at all. "Selling Grandpa's house out from under you, shutting off his phone number, packing your whole childhood down to four boxes for a tiny studio—you deserved way better than this for turning eighteen."

Gwen pulled back just enough to cup both sides of my face in her palms, looking straight into my eyes with fierce, unmistakable joy shining right underneath our shared grief.

"Listen to me, Nolan Lennox," my eighteen-year-old sister said firmly. "All year long while Tammy had you trapped over in that awful trailer off Route 13, I used to lie awake down this hall terrified I had lost my big brother forever. Grandpa got to see me turn eighteen knowing you came back for me. Selling brick and drywall doesn't scare me one bit—I get my brother back, she can never touch you again, and tomorrow night I actually get to move in with you! Now hand me the utility stack and open my laptop; you're not carrying one more phone call by yourself."

True to her word, Gwen booted up her clunky gray laptop running Quantum OS 98 right there on an upturned packing crate beside me, drafted a neat checklist of final meter-read dates while leaning warm against my shoulder, and refused to go to sleep until every single envelope was stacked, stamped, and conquered together.

### Part 3: Moving Into a Rectangular Box and Quantum OS 2000

#### 4:15 PM — Eleven Feet by Eighteen Feet of Pure Freedom

The afternoon the estate realtor hung the *UNDER CONTRACT* rider on Grandpa Walter's front lawn and Nolan unlocked the door of Studio 2B for me to move in officially, I took one look inside his tiny rectangular apartment, burst out laughing with pure happiness, and hugged my brother right in the doorway.

Nolan hadn't exaggerated by half an inch: Studio 2B was an eleven-by-eighteen-foot rectangular shoebox designed strictly for one solitary tenant who didn't own two manual wheelchairs.

Once we rode the groaning lobby elevator up to the second floor—the very last month that ancient elevator worked before the landlord taped a yellow out-of-order notice across the call button—and parked both of our folded manual wheelchairs neatly near the entry where they still left a clear roll to the kitchen and a straight view of the television, you could see our entire new life in a single sweep. Flush in the far left corner against two perpendicular walls beneath a small beige window air-conditioner sat Nolan's fifty-four-inch full-sized bed, draped immediately in Grandpa Walter's dark-green and navy-blue plaid wool blanket to double as our bed by night and our only couch by day. Directly across the narrow strip of clear wood floor on the opposite wall stood Nolan's scuffed oak dresser—now split evenly, top two drawers mine, bottom two drawers his—topped by a chunky nineteen-inch television with V-shaped metal rabbit ears and a brushed-silver dual-deck VCR and DVD combo unit swiveled cleanly toward the corner bed so Nolan and I could watch movies together propped against the pillows or seated in our wheelchairs. Along the unobstructed back-right wall ran the tiniest functional kitchen in Virginia: a dorm-style mini-fridge tucked underneath the counter with clear floor space to swing the door wide, a narrow single sink, a compact natural-gas oven, and Nolan's white microwave perched on a rolling wire cart whose lower wire shelves doubled as our entire pantry of canned soup, rolled oats, peanut butter, and macaroni boxes.

Beside the entry hooks sat a pine bathroom door barely three feet wide.

"Okay, it's TINY," Nolan winced sheepishly on his matte-silver forearm crutches, glancing from the rolling microwave pantry cart to the single corner bed as old reflexes from Tammy's put-downs tried to hunch his shoulders inward. "I know you're six-foot-one, Gwenny, and sharing one full bed and two dresser drawers after having your own room your whole life is—"

I didn't even let him finish the apology. Stepping across the narrow strip of floor on my matte-charcoal crutches, I wrapped both arms around my five-foot-seven brother and rested my chin happily on top of his tousled brown head.

"Nolan, stop," I smiled, giving him a tight squeeze. "Tammy isn't here anymore, Grandpa's mortgage bank is paid off once closing clears, and I'm living with my favorite person in the universe! Show me where the lease addendum is so I can put my name next to yours right now!"

Two minutes later, sitting cross-legged side by side on top of Grandpa Walter's plaid blanket on the corner full-sized bed, Nolan handed me a blue ballpoint pen off the microwave cart and watched proudly as I printed *Gwendolyn M. Lennox* in the co-tenant box right beside *Nolan W. Lennox*. For an eighteen-year-old who had just buried her grandfather and packed up her childhood house, seeing our two names linked in ink on one front-door lease felt better than winning the lottery.

***

#### 8:45 PM — Three-Foot Bathrooms, Learning Not to Hide, and Quantum OS 2000 Professional

Moving in together as two grown siblings inside a one-room rectangle came with exactly two immediate engineering puzzles—and we solved both before midnight.

The first puzzle announced itself right after supper when I carried my sleep shirt into the three-foot bathroom to change out of my jeans.
