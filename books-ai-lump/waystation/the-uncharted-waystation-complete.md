# Book One: Beyond the Studio Rift

## Chapter 1: Two Names on One Studio Lease

### Part 1: Aisle Four, Cutback Transfers, and Ready for Everything

**Date:** 1998-10-24 at 12:15 America/New_York

![At a sunlit corner table along the brass-and-glass mezzanine railing of the upper-level Food Court inside Emerald Creek Mall beneath its white peaked fabric tent canopy and skylights, twenty-four-year-old Tammy (five-foot-nothing, straight jet-black hair swept behind her shoulders, pastel baby-blue zip-up fleece cardigan pulled open off her shoulders over a modest white ribbed camisole) perches forward on her chair, arches her back, and puffs her chest out across the table as she smiles boldly at twenty-one-year-old Nolan Lennox (five-foot-seven, wearing his royal-blue Blue Tech polo shirt after his morning shift), who grins back innocently over a French fry without looking down from her face while his two single-tube matte-silver Lofstrand forearm crutches lean cleanly beside his chair on the warm terracotta quarry tile floor.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/e001/e001_c001_p001_emerald_creek_food_court_day1.jpg)

#### 12:15 PM — Seventy-Five Ohms and Coaxial Peg Hooks

Fifteen minutes before the end of my Saturday morning shift in Aisle Four at the Emerald Creek Blue Tech, every blister pack of seventy-five-ohm coaxial cable splitters hung flush with the front plastic lip of its peg hook, sorted in ascending order from three-foot patch leads on the left to twenty-five-foot weather-booted runs on the right.

Home-theater cables were my favorite aisle in the whole superstore because hardware specifications never said one thing while secretly meaning the opposite. A threaded F-connector either mated cleanly onto a television tuner jack or it did not. Nobody in Aisle Four dropped unannounced hints, rolled their eyes when I took a literal question at face value, or got impatient if it took my brain two extra seconds to translate noisy retail small talk into plain English. After turning twenty-one that spring and filling out paper applications at eleven different storefronts within crutching distance of Grandpa Walter's two-story house so I wouldn't even need a bus pass to get to work, Blue Tech had been the one place whose floor manager listened to me rattle off memory-bus speeds and dual-deck VCR hookups without caring that my hazel eyes tracked slightly past his ear or that I walked on matte-silver Lofstrand forearm crutches.

Shifting my weight onto my left silver forearm cuff, I straightened one crooked yellow price tag beside the indoor dipole antennas just as a soft, polite voice spoke behind my elbow.

"Excuse me—sorry to bother you, sir, do you know if the cheap rabbit ears actually pick up anything inside a metal trailer?"

Turning smoothly on my rubber crutch tips, I looked forward at chest height first—and then had to angle my chin noticeably downward.

Even standing in thick, chunky white late-nineties platform sneakers designed to boost her off the linoleum, the young woman smiling up at me couldn't have stood an inch over five feet flat. At five-foot-seven, I was almost never the tall person in a room—my kid sister Gwen had caught me eye-to-eye back in middle school and towered over me at six-foot-one now—yet I stood a good seven inches taller than this customer. Glossy, ruler-straight jet-black hair fell past the shoulders of her soft pastel baby-blue zip-up fleece cardigan, framing a round, rosy-cheeked baby face that made me figure she was twenty or twenty-one like me. Beneath dark lashes, big warm doe-brown eyes met mine with a gentle, unhurried smile, and she didn't flick her gaze down toward the gray open forearm cuffs around my sleeves for even half a second.

"No bother at all—that's actually a really good physics question," I said earnestly, tapping the nine-dollar cardboard box on the bottom peg instead of the forty-dollar amplified dish corporate wanted us pushing on endcaps. "Corrugated aluminum siding on a mobile home acts almost like a Faraday cage against UHF and VHF broadcast waves. Even an expensive amplified antenna won't pull a clean picture if you set it on top of a television against an interior wall. What actually works is buying this plain nine-dollar passive dipole set plus a twelve-foot RG-six coaxial jumper cable for four bucks so you can set the antenna base right on your windowsill against the glass."

Her warm brown eyes widened as if I had just handed her a magic trick instead of saved her twenty-seven dollars.

"Really? You're not even gonna try talking me into the expensive box?" she asked softly, clasping both small hands around the nine-dollar carton. "Thank you so much. Honestly, half the time clerks talk to me like I'm twelve just because I'm short, and I rode the fifteen bus all the way over from Robert Hall Boulevard this morning just to get my TV working."

My whole face lit up at the route number. Bus schedules were the second half of how my brain mapped Tidewater Virginia.

"Wait—from Robert Hall Boulevard to Emerald Creek?" I said, grinning before I could stop myself. "The fifteen doesn't even run single-seat between Robert Hall and here! You had to ride the northbound fifteen toward Naval Station Norfolk up to Military Highway and Old Emerald Creek Road and do the cutback transfer onto the southbound fifteen turning left toward the mall!"

She let out a delighted, musical laugh that crinkled the corners of her round cheeks. "Oh my gosh, yes! Stand out by eight lanes of highway traffic watching cars fly past just to hop on another bus going two minutes down the road! Nobody ever knows how annoying that transfer is unless they ride the bus themselves."

"I walk four blocks from my grandpa's house right behind Emerald Creek Mall," I explained happily as we headed up the center racetrack toward Register Three, my silver forearm crutches clicking a steady, comfortable rhythm beside her platform sneakers, "though anytime my sister Gwen and I head up toward Glenrock Mall or Norfolk with our wheelchairs, we ride the fifteen constantly. I'm Nolan, by the way."

"I'm Tammy," she said warmly, setting the antenna box and twelve-foot coaxial coil gently onto my checkout belt as my twelve-thirty relief cashier stepped up beside the register drawer to take over my lane. Ringing up her thirteen-dollar total and slipping the receipt neatly inside her plastic bag, I glanced down at my digital wristwatch: twelve-twenty-nine. Right on schedule. Four blocks home put me on Grandpa Walter's porch by twelve-forty-five, though Gwen was working through astronomy problem sets at the kitchen table until two and Grandpa didn't take his afternoon heart pills until one-thirty.

Tammy didn't turn toward the automatic exit doors when I handed her the bag. Shifting the plastic handles into her left hand, she tilted her chin up with the most polite, inviting smile I had ever had aimed at me in twenty-one years.

"Are you getting off work right now, Nolan?" Tammy asked gently.

"Yes, ma'am—just finished my morning four-hour shift," I said, unclipping my laminated Blue Tech name badge from my royal-blue polo collar and slipping it into my jeans pocket. "Walking home right past the mall across the street."

"Would you maybe want to grab lunch with me upstairs at the Emerald Creek Mall food court on your way home?" Tammy asked softly, cheeks pinking in a way that looked almost shy. "You just saved me twenty-seven dollars on my antenna—let me at least buy you some fries."

For two full seconds my brain stalled flat between gears while I ran the math. Nobody outside Grandpa Walter or Gwen had ever invited me to eat lunch with them—not in high school, and definitely not a pretty girl with warm brown eyes and polite manners. Checking my mental clock twice—forty-five minutes across Old Emerald Creek Road still got me up Grandpa's front steps by quarter past one, fifteen minutes ahead of Grandpa's medicine routine—I nodded with a slightly crooked, genuine grin.

"Sure," I said. "I'd really like that, Tammy."

***

#### 12:50 PM — Terracotta Tile and Missing Every Hint

Ten minutes later, after crossing the crosswalk from the Blue Tech lot into Emerald Creek Mall and riding the freestanding cream-and-brass scenic glass elevator opposite Wellington's narrow recessed portal flush up to the second-floor terracotta-tile landing, we carried two red plastic trays from Crown Burger over to a quiet square two-top table tucked along a diagonal side wing of the polished brass-and-glass mezzanine railing.

Overhead, bright October daylight poured down through perimeter skylights beneath the high white peaked fabric tent canopy of the food court, warming the eight-by-eight reddish-brown quarry tiles under our shoes while the smell of salted fries and cinnamon sugar from Annie's Pretzels drifted past. Propping both of my matte-silver Lofstrand forearm crutches neatly parallel against the right side of my chair frame where nobody walking along the railing could trip over the gray rubber tips, I sat down across from Tammy, lined my paper napkin parallel to the edge of my tray, and unwrapped my burger.

Tammy proved to be the easiest person to talk to I had ever met. Whenever most neurotypical people made conversation, it felt like trying to catch tennis balls thrown two at a time in the dark—jokes that weren't actually jokes, questions where the honest answer turned out to be rude. Tammy didn't rush me once. She rested her chin lightly on her palm, sipped from her plain yellow fountain cup, and listened with rapt, polite attention while I told her how Grandpa Walter—seventy-six and still healthy as an ox, retired Navy machinist, sharpest neurotypical common sense in Chesapeake—had stepped into Juvenile and Domestic Relations Court back in the eighties to take full custody of my sister Gwen and me when we were kids, and how Gwen was razor-sharp at math and orbital astronomy and basically my best friend in the whole world.

"That's really sweet, Nolan," Tammy said warmly, smiling across the laminate tabletop without me ever thinking to mention Gwen's age or how tall she stood. "Not a lot of guys talk that nicely about their family."

Setting her cup aside, Tammy reached up with both hands and swept all of her straight, glossy jet-black hair cleanly back behind her shoulders. Next she slid her pastel baby-blue zip-up fleece cardigan wide open, tugging both blue fleece sleeves partway back behind her elbows as she scooted forward onto the very front edge of her chair. Planting both small hands back against the curves of her jeans hips, she arched her lower spine sharply inward, drew her shoulders tight behind her ribcage, and puffed her chest prominently out over the edge of the table inside her fitted white ribbed cotton camisole.

Locking her big, warm doe-brown eyes straight onto mine across our red plastic trays, the very first thing Tammy said was low, steady, and deliberate.

"Just so you know, Nolan... I'm ready for everything."

I didn't glance down at her chest—not once. For one thing, Grandpa Walter had raised me since elementary school to look people respectfully in the face when they spoke to me; for another, my brain had zero wiring for invisible body-language codes, so I didn't have the faintest clue a guy sitting across a mall table was *supposed* to notice why she had arched her back and puffed her chest out like that. If I registered her posture at all, I figured the curved metal rungs on Emerald Creek Mall's food-court chairs were digging into her spine.

Better yet, inside my literal-minded twenty-one-year-old head, her words made complete, wonderful sense right on top of what I had just finished explaining thirty seconds earlier.

*Ready for everything.* She meant my crutches. She meant my manual wheelchair sitting folded in Grandpa's hallway for long outings, waiting thirty seconds on hydraulic bus lifts, riding CVTA transfers instead of owning a car, and knowing Gwen and Grandpa Walter came packaged with my life.

Warm, uncomplicated gratitude washed straight through my chest. Still holding my French fry halfway to my mouth, keeping my gaze politely on her smiling eyes, I grinned from ear to ear.

"Honestly, Tammy, thank you for saying that," I said earnestly. "You have no idea how rare that is. Usually the second somebody realizes crutches aren't for a sprained ankle that heals in two weeks—or that going anywhere far means planning around wheelchair-lift buses—they get real polite and suddenly remember somewhere else they need to be. Knowing none of that bothers you takes a huge weight off."

Across the narrow wood-grain tabletop, Tammy stayed frozen in that arched, shoulders-back pose for two full heartbeats, blinking once as if waiting for me to wink or laugh—before realizing I was one hundred percent serious and had no earthly idea she had just offered to ride back to her trailer off Route 13 and climb into bed with me that very afternoon.

Slowly, an amused, almost fascinated softness curved the corners of her mouth. Letting her shoulders relax an inch forward without losing a drop of that warm, inviting glow in her doe-brown eyes, Tammy pulled a ballpoint pen out of her purse and wrote seven neat digits across the corner of a clean paper napkin.

"You really are the sweetest guy in Chesapeake, Nolan Lennox," Tammy murmured, sliding the folded napkin right beside my red tray. "What evening are you off in the next couple days? Come over to my place off Robert Hall—I'll cook you dinner and show you how well my new antenna works."

"I'm off Monday afternoon," I said happily, tucking the napkin safely into my pocket beside my Blue Tech badge. "Grandpa and Gwen can swing by in Grandpa's car after dinner to pick me up."

Walking the four quiet blocks home from Emerald Creek Mall at one-fifteen in the afternoon—fifteen minutes ahead of Grandpa Walter's pills, right on routine, sun warm on my royal-blue polo sleeves and silver crutches clicking steady down the sidewalk—I couldn't stop smiling to myself, certain I had just met the gentlest, most polite girl in the world.

### Part 2: Four Wooden Steps, Sofa Cuddles, and Your Older Sister?

**Date:** 1998-10-26 at 16:30 America/New_York

![At golden October dusk on the gravel driveway outside Tammy's beige-and-white single-wide mobile home with four wooden steps, taller sixteen-year-old Gwen Lennox (six-foot-one, in a modest burgundy pullover and jeans) wraps her shorter twenty-one-year-old brother Nolan Lennox (five-foot-seven, in a blue plaid flannel shirt and jeans) in their trademark warm greeting hug beside all four of their single-tube matte-silver and matte-charcoal Lofstrand forearm crutches leaning against their healthy seventy-six-year-old grandfather Walter Lennox's parked sedan, while five-foot-nothing Tammy stands at the foot of the wooden steps in her baby-blue cardigan and platform sneakers tilting her head way up in wide-eyed surprise to ask if Gwen is Nolan's older sister, as Grandpa Walter smiles warmly beside the car.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/e001/e001_c001_p002_older_sister_trailer_pickup.jpg)

#### 4:30 PM — Four Steep Steps and What Cuddling Literally Means

Four steep pressure-treated wooden steps led up from the rutted gravel lane of Pinecrest Court to the front-middle aluminum storm door of Tammy's rusty old single-wide trailer, which was the exact reason my manual wheelchair was sitting folded back in Grandpa Walter's front foyer near Emerald Creek Mall while I walked in from Robert Hall Boulevard on my matte-silver Lofstrand forearm crutches.

Tammy had warned me politely about those steps on the phone Sunday night so I wouldn't get surprised at the curb—a small, thoughtful detail my rule-oriented brain appreciated more than flowers. Riding CVTA Route 15 out from Emerald Creek on a mild October Monday afternoon had taken the full cutback transfer in reverse: catching the northbound fifteen outside the mall up Old Emerald Creek Road to eight-lane Military Highway, crossing carefully on the walk signal, and boarding the southbound fifteen all the way down to Robert Hall Boulevard before crutching five minutes past gravel lots and weathered mobile-home skirting.

Before I even got my right knuckles raised to knock, the aluminum screen door swung inward and Tammy smiled down at me from the doorway.

Without her chunky white platform sneakers, standing completely barefoot on the patterned beige linoleum in medium-wash jeans and a simple white ribbed cotton spaghetti-strap camisole, Tammy looked even smaller than she had at Blue Tech—five feet flat to the millimeter, the crown of her glossy, straight jet-black hair barely clearing my collarbone as I climbed the four wooden treads onto her threshold and clicked my silver crutches neatly inside.

"You made it!" Tammy beamed, stepping back between the living room on the left and the kitchenette on the right so I had plenty of room to plant both gray rubber tips on the flat linoleum without bumping the doorframe. It was unmistakably a weathered trailer-park rental—rusty exterior seams, yellowed acoustic ceiling panels bearing overlapping rings where the park landlord had patched roof leaks over and over again, and the faint ghost of stale cigarette smoke left behind in the dark wood paneling by whatever tenant had lived here before Tammy moved in recently, since Tammy didn't smoke and nobody in my family, not even Grandpa Walter, had ever touched a cigarette—yet Tammy had scrubbed every counter spotless. "Look over by the front window first—tell me if I hooked it up right!"

Right on the narrow wood-paneled living-room windowsill to the left of the front door, across the room from a white twenty-inch plastic box fan humming on low speed in the rear dinette window because the trailer had no air-conditioning, sat the nine-dollar passive rabbit-ear antenna and twelve-foot coaxial jumper cable from Aisle Four. Over against the far left end wall on a small laminate stand, her thirteen-inch television was pulling in afternoon local news without a single bar of static snow.

"Zero ghosting on channel thirteen," I said happily, inspecting the threaded F-connector finger-tight on the tuner jack. "You routed the coax clean clear of the fan motor too so sixty-hertz interference wouldn't bleed into the picture."

Tammy laughed softly, big warm doe-brown eyes crinkling in her round baby face, and handed me a tall plastic tumbler of sweet iced tea beaded cold with condensation.

"Come sit on the couch and cuddle with me while we talk, Nolan," Tammy said gently, nodding toward her soft floral thrift-store sofa against the wood-paneled wall.

If she had used some vague romantic hint, it would have sailed clean past my ears just like Saturday's lunch line had. *Sit on the couch and cuddle while we talk*, though, was plain, literal English—and cuddling on a couch was something my autistic brain already knew and trusted completely from a lifetime of curling up shoulder-to-shoulder under wool blankets with Gwen on Grandpa's queen-sized pull-out sofa bed downstairs at home, ever since we were little kids visiting Grandpa's un-adapted two-story house from our parents' one-story place and still sharing that sofa bed whenever our legs weren't feeling up to climbing the stairs. Propping both of my matte-silver forearm crutches parallel against the end table within easy reach, I lowered myself onto the left cushion, set my iced tea on a coaster, and lifted my right arm openly so Tammy could settle in beside me.

Tammy didn't push a single thing beyond those exact words.

Tucking both bare feet comfortably underneath her on the center cushion, she leaned gently against my blue plaid flannel shirt, rested her cheek right against my shoulder, and just talked with me like we had all afternoon in the world. Whenever my voice launched into a ten-minute tangent about motherboard bus speeds or why CVTA timed the Glenrock Mall pulse transfers at fifteen and forty-five past the hour, she didn't check the clock, didn't tease me for sounding like an instruction manual, and didn't interrupt once. In quiet, matter-of-fact sentences of her own, she told me she was twenty-four—three years older than me, which startled me because her round face looked barely twenty—and explained how she got by carefully month to month on SSI, Food Stamps, and Medicaid, keeping her bills tiny in this trailer because clocking too many payroll hours anywhere could make the government yank her medical card out from under her overnight.

Around quarter to six, Tammy padded barefoot three paces into the narrow wood-paneled kitchenette, unfolded a small plastic step-stool so her five-foot-nothing reach could pull down a box of spaghetti and a jar of marinara from the upper cabinet above the sink, and boiled dinner right there on her narrow four-burner stove while garlic butter toasted in the oven. Eating across her tiny two-chair formica dinette with the window fan humming softly beside us and zero raised voices anywhere in the room, my shoulders felt looser than they had ever felt inside a stranger's house.

***

#### 7:10 PM — One Greeting Hug and Two Approvals

At ten minutes past seven, twin yellow headlights swept unhurriedly down the gravel lane of Pinecrest Court and eased to a stop beside Tammy's yard, tires crunching quiet over fallen autumn oak leaves.

Right on the dot—because Grandpa Walter respected schedules almost as religiously as I did—Grandpa's clean, well-kept silver-tan four-door sedan idled smooth in park. At seventy-six years old, Grandpa Walter didn't have an ounce of quit or frailty in him: sharp behind the wheel after dark, steady on his feet in his tan canvas windbreaker and wire-rimmed glasses, still changing his own oil in the two-car garage and fixing hinges around our spacious two-story Emerald Creek house every Saturday.

Slipping into her pastel baby-blue zip-up fleece cardigan against the October dusk breeze and stepping into her chunky white platform sneakers by the door, Tammy followed me politely out onto the top landing while I navigated the four wooden steps one steady tread at a time on my silver forearm crutches—never grabbing my elbow without asking, just standing ready until both of my sneakers met level gravel.

It was Tammy's very first time laying eyes on either Grandpa Walter or Gwen.

Before Grandpa had even finished rounding the back bumper with a friendly wave, the front passenger door swung wide and Gwen stepped effortlessly out onto the gravel with her two tall matte-charcoal Lofstrand forearm crutches.

Seeing my sister after even half a day apart triggered the exact same automatic reflex it had since she was in kindergarten: Nolan and Gwen Lennox *always* greeted each other with a hug. Stepping three strides forward to meet her beside the rear fender, Gwen and I propped all four of our forearm crutches—my shorter matte-silver pair on the left, her taller matte-charcoal pair on the right—cleanly side by side against the sedan's quarter panel. Opening both arms wide, I pulled my sister straight into a tight, warm bear hug, my five-foot-seven chin tucking right into its familiar spot below her collarbone while six-foot-one Gwen wrapped both arms snugly around my shoulders and rested her cheek happily on top of my tousled brown hair.

"Hey, big head," Gwen murmured warmly against my hair, giving me an extra squeeze before we stepped half a pace apart, still smiling. "Have a good dinner?"

"Really good—spaghetti and garlic toast," I grinned, turning halfway toward the wooden trailer steps to introduce them. "Grandpa, Gwenny, this is Tammy."

At the bottom of the four wooden steps, Tammy had stopped completely still on the gravel with both small hands clasped in front of her baby-blue cardigan.

Even standing in her thick white platform soles, five-foot-nothing Tammy had to tilt her round face sharply upward—past my five-foot-seven shoulder, and then another full six inches higher—just to meet Gwen's green-hazel eyes beneath wavy honey-brown curls that towered thirteen inches above Tammy's crown. Blinking twice in genuine, wide-eyed astonishment at the height difference she had just watched in that hug, Tammy smiled up politely.

"Hi! Oh—wait," Tammy asked gently, looking back and forth from my chin to the top of Gwen's head. "Is Gwen your older sister?"

Gwen let out a bright, easy laugh without a trace of self-consciousness, while a proud grin split my face from ear to ear.

"Nope," I said happily. "She's my younger sister. Gwenny's sixteen—five years behind me."

For half a heartbeat Tammy looked almost dizzied craning her neck that far up at a high-school sophomore—realizing on the spot that Nolan's *kid* sister stood over a full foot taller than a twenty-four-year-old grown woman—yet she smoothed it seamlessly into the sweetest, most respectful manners imaginable. Stepping briskly across the gravel, Tammy shook Grandpa Walter's calloused machinist hand with both of hers, thanked him politely for driving all the way out toward Robert Hall Boulevard after dark, and told Gwen warmly how proudly I had bragged about her astronomy math at the mall food court on Saturday.

"Pleasure meeting you, Tammy," Grandpa Walter said in his steady baritone, giving a small, unmistakable nod as his sharp neurotypical eyes took in the swept porch steps, the working porch light she had switched on so Gwen and I could see our footing on the gravel, and how unhurriedly she stood talking with us by the car. "Thank you for feeding this boy something besides mall french fries."

"Anytime at all, Mr. Lennox—drive safe getting home," Tammy called softly from the foot of her steps, waving until I had settled into the front passenger seat, Gwen had stretched her long legs comfortably across the back bench behind me, and all four of our Lofstrand forearm crutches lay parallel across the rear floorboard.

Backing smoothly out onto the paved lane toward Robert Hall Boulevard with warm dashboard amber glowing over the steering column, Grandpa Walter glanced once into the rearview mirror and nodded to himself.

"Polite young woman, Nolan," Grandpa said plainly. "Good manners, clean yard, and she waited till you were steady on the gravel before saying a word. Seems like a nice kid."

My shoulders warmed—yet before letting myself celebrate, I turned halfway around over the cloth headrest toward the back seat. As much as I trusted Grandpa Walter, in my mind Gwen's verdict mattered more than anybody else's on Earth. If neurotypical Gwen heard one false note behind someone's voice, I didn't want anything to do with them.

In the glow of a passing streetlight, sixteen-year-old Gwen met my eyes and broke into a genuinely happy, unmistakable smile.

"I really like her, Nolan," Gwen said warmly, reaching forward to squeeze my shoulder once over the seatback. "Even when she thought I was twenty-five, she was super sweet about it, and I haven't seen you look that relaxed around somebody new in forever."

Turning back toward the windshield as Grandpa steered us up toward Military Highway and home to Emerald Creek, my chest felt lighter than air. Grandpa Walter approved, Gwen approved, and nobody had yelled, rushed, or expected me to read minds all evening long.

### Part 3: Booth Fries, First Night Away From Home, and Connecting Day One

**Date:** 1998-10-31 at 13:45 America/New_York

![Inside a sunlit afternoon window booth with burgundy vinyl seats and warm wood-grain trim at the freestanding Crown Burger on South Military Highway in Chesapeake, Tammy (five-foot-nothing, straight jet-black hair past her shoulders, pastel baby-blue zip-up fleece cardigan over a white ribbed camisole) leans warmly on her elbows across plain off-white fountain cups and burger-and-fries baskets smiling at Nolan Lennox (five-foot-seven, wearing his royal-blue Blue Tech polo shirt after his Saturday morning shift), while both of Nolan's single-tube matte-silver Lofstrand forearm crutches lean cleanly against the wooden booth frame beside him.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/e001/e001_c001_p003_crown_burger_south_military.jpg)

#### 1:45 PM — South Military Highway, Booth Fries, and Uncharted Territory

Afternoon October sunlight slanted warm across burgundy vinyl bench cushions and wood-grain laminate inside the freestanding Crown Burger on South Military Highway, glinting off the matte-silver aluminum shafts of my two Lofstrand forearm crutches propped neatly against the end of our window booth.

Exactly one week had passed since Tammy walked up behind my elbow in Aisle Four at Blue Tech asking about rabbit-ear antennas—and five days since Grandpa Walter and Gwen drove out to pick me up after Monday night's spaghetti dinner. Clocking out of my Saturday morning shift at twelve-thirty, I had kept my royal-blue work polo on, caught the northbound CVTA Route 15 outside Emerald Creek Mall up to eight-lane Military Highway, crossed on the pedestrian signal, and ridden the southbound fifteen all the way down toward Robert Hall Boulevard to meet Tammy on her side of Chesapeake.

Across our two plain off-white fountain cups and plastic baskets of crinkle-cut fries, Tammy rested both forearms on the table inside her pastel baby-blue zip-up fleece cardigan, straight jet-black hair framing her round cheeks while her warm doe-brown eyes crinkled at me like I was the best company in Tidewater.

"I still can't get over Monday night," Tammy laughed softly, dipping a fry into ketchup. "Here I am squinting out into my driveway expecting some little kid in braces, and out steps your *younger* sister towering a whole head over both of us like a college volleyball star. Your grandpa is such a gentleman, too."

"Grandpa and Gwenny both really liked you," I said earnestly, warming right up at getting to deliver a one-hundred-percent literal, verified report. "In the car on the way back to Emerald Creek, Grandpa said you had good manners and waited till my crutches were steady on the gravel, and Gwenny said she hadn't seen me look that relaxed around somebody new in forever."

Tammy's smile softened in a way that made my ears feel warm. Taking a slow sip from her straw, she tilted her head slightly across the booth.

"Are you scheduled to open tomorrow morning at Blue Tech, Nolan, or did they give you Sunday off?"

"Off all day Sunday," I said happily. "Don't go back till Monday afternoon."

"Then walk back over to my place with me after we finish eating," Tammy said gently, reaching halfway across the laminate tabletop to brush her fingertips lightly against my knuckles. "We can put a movie tape in the VCR, order a pizza later, and instead of making your seventy-six-year-old grandpa drive all the way down Military Highway in the dark a second time this week... you could just spend the night at my place and catch the Sunday bus home whenever you wake up."

For three full seconds my chewing stopped dead while every scheduling gear inside my head spun at double speed.

At twenty-one years old, I had never spent a single night anywhere on Earth that wasn't with my family.

Not once. Back when Gwen and I were small and still living with our parents in a one-story house, an overnight trip meant visiting Grandpa Walter's big two-story house near Emerald Creek Mall—where Grandpa unfolded the queen-sized pull-out sofa bed in the downstairs family room near the first-floor full bathroom and tucked Gwen and me in side by side under his wool blanket so our legs wouldn't have to climb the stairs at bedtime. After Grandpa stepped into court to take custody of us, every single night since had been spent either up in my own full-sized bed across the upstairs hall from Gwen and Grandpa, or right back down on that familiar queen sofa bed beside Gwen whenever our legs ached. Other kids went to noisy neighborhood sleepovers or Boy Scout tents; between forearm crutches, wheelchair logistics, and how quickly unfamiliar houses drained my autistic battery, home had been my sole overnight coordinates for twenty-one straight years.

Naturally, my literal brain jumped straight to practical checklist items instead of anything unspoken.

"Oh—uh, I didn't pack a toothbrush or sleep clothes," I blurted honestly, glancing down at my royal-blue Blue Tech polo and jeans. "And if I don't walk through the front door before supper, Grandpa and Gwenny are gonna think a car clipped me crossing Military Highway."

Tammy didn't laugh at me or act like calling home at twenty-one was childish. Instead, she smiled warmly as she stacked our empty fry baskets onto one tray.

"I keep unopened spare toothbrushes under my bathroom sink, a clean t-shirt and gym shorts aren't a problem if you just sleep in your undershirt, and my corded phone hangs right on the kitchen wall," Tammy said smoothly. "You can call your grandpa the minute we get inside."

Checking my mental rulebook twice—Sunday off work, zero night driving for Grandpa, clear phone check-in, first-run Sunday fifteen bus timetable already memorized inside my head—I nodded and reached for my silver forearm crutches beside the booth.

"Okay," I smiled, pulse beating a little faster under my collar. "Let's do that."

***

#### 4:15 PM — Wall-Phone Check-In, Clinic Paperwork, and Turning Out the Lamp

Walking the quiet stretch from South Military Highway into Pinecrest Court took ten unhurried minutes in the mild October sun, my rubber crutch tips clicking steady along the asphalt shoulder beside Tammy's chunky white platform sneakers before I climbed the four steep wooden steps up to her front-middle aluminum storm door.

Stepping inside between the living room on the left and the kitchenette on the right—where yellowed ceiling panels still showed faint rings from leaks the trailer-park landlord had tar-patched over and over again, and a ghost of the previous tenant's old cigarette smoke lingered softly beneath Tammy's lemon pine cleaner—Tammy slipped out of her platform sneakers and blue cardigan barefoot on the patterned beige linoleum and pointed straight to the beige corded phone mounted on the wood-paneled partition wall.

#### 7:15 AM, The Next Morning — The Quiet Room and Ten Raised Seals

Back on Earth, walking half a mile on forearm crutches without our wheelchairs meant dodging buckled concrete, racing flashing red pedestrian timers across four-lane avenues, and enduring strangers asking invasive medical questions before breakfast.

***

#### 7:45 PM — The Seventeenth Archway Is Our Front Door

Even more astonishing was what woke directly behind the Astrolabe, carved straight into the living cliffside wall of the Waystation itself.

A seventeenth archway—wider than any apartment doorway on Earth and inlaid with warm turquoise and amber crystals—filled with a gentle, translucent curtain of golden starlight that rippled softly without a heavy wooden door, brass latch, or raised sill anywhere at its base.

"Keepers' Hall," Archivist Thorne said gently, gesturing toward the glowing threshold. "Built by our Founders for those who tend the rings—so you never again have to choose between an open world and a warm hearth."

Side by side on our four forearm crutches, Gwen and I walked straight through the Glowing Hearth-Archway. The curtain of warm starlight parted around our shoulders like silk—sealing out the mountain night breeze while never asking either of us to let go of a crutch grip.

Both of us stopped in the warm cedar light and let out a simultaneous, breathless laugh of disbelief.

Every cramped, humiliating hardship of our eleven-by-eighteen-foot studio box back in Chesapeake had its answer waiting right in front of us. Beneath our bare feet, geothermal rose-quartz flagstones radiated gentle warmth like a sunlit hearthstone. Semicircular mossy-wool couches curved around a crackling stone fireplace with custom-carved cedar notches built right into every armrest to hold four Lofstrand forearm crutches upright within effortless reach. To our right stood a step-free stone-and-copper kitchen with knee-clearance seated prep counters so neither of us ever had to cook standing on crutches, beside a cold snowmelt spring-larder stocked with fresh loaves, orchard plums, and cheeses. Sweeping rose-quartz ramps—zero stairs anywhere—linked a spacious, curb-free ninety-eight-degree travertine soaking bath to a crystal-domed Moon-Glass Observatory overhead.

And tucked beneath a wide twin-moon skylight in the warmest timber alcove sat an oversized, transfer-height carved cedar canopy bed piled deep with cloud-wool quilts—wide enough for two grown siblings to stretch out without bumping elbows, yet cozy enough that Gwen immediately turned to me and wrapped both arms tight around my neck.

"Even with a whole cliffside keep, shortstop," my nineteen-year-old sister whispered fiercely against my hair, "nobody's putting me down some empty hall away from my brother. Grandpa's blanket goes right on that bed."

My chest felt fuller than I thought ribs could hold—until one lingering tug of big-brother responsibility made me glance back through the Glowing Hearth-Archway toward the sixteenth outer portal across the courtyard, where a familiar faint amber shimmer showed the scuffed wood floorboards of Studio 2B in Chesapeake.

"Now that the rings are awake," Archivist Thorne noted kindly from the courtyard threshold, "your homeward doorway answers your hands in either direction whenever you wish."

Gwen and I traded one long look. Thinking of two days in the crags—meaning Sunday evening back in Chesapeake, a missed Saturday shift at Blue Tech, and Grandpa Walter's silver-framed photo sitting alone beside our parked wheelchairs and laptops—I squeezed Gwen's hand.

"Let's step back for five minutes," I said quietly, bracing myself for angry voicemail lights on our corded phone, "grab Grandpa's photo and our things before the landlord locks us out, and come right back."

### Part 2: Nine-Fifteen on the VCR Clock and Choosing Home

#### 9:15 PM — Not One Second Gone in Chesapeake

Stepping hand in hand with Nolan through the amber archway back onto the scuffed wood strip of Studio 2B in Emerald Creek, I braced myself for stale weekend air, a blinking red voicemail light from Nolan's Blue Tech manager, or a yellow late-notice slip wedged under our front door.

Instead, October rain was still pattering the exact same rhythm against our silent window air-conditioner above the corner full-sized bed.

Halfway between the rolling microwave-cart pantry and the oak dresser, both Nolan and I stopped dead on our bare feet and four forearm crutches, staring straight across the eleven-by-eighteen-foot rectangle at the glowing green LED clock on the front of Nolan's brushed-silver VCR and DVD combo unit.

*9:15.*

My breath caught in my throat. On the milk-crate nightstand beside our rumpled pillows, the screen of my open Quantum OS 2000 Professional laptop hadn't even gone into its ten-minute sleep saver—my two glowing moons were still mid-orbit right beside *9:15 PM* in the bottom-right corner of the taskbar, and the beige corded speakerphone receiver still felt faintly warm from when my thumb had clicked Regional Mobility Supply off forty-eight hours of Vaeloria travel ago!

"Nolan," I whispered, grabbing my brother's sleeve with wide, shining eyes. "Look at the VCR clock. Look at my taskbar!"

Nolan blinked hard from the green *9:15* digits to the raindrop clinging unmoved to the window glass above his pillow, and a slow, amazed laugh broke right out of his chest. "Time stood completely still," he breathed, the last ounce of missed-shift dread vanishing off his shoulders in an instant. "We didn't miss Saturday. Nobody worried. Earth literally waited right on the minute we left!"

Looking around our tiny rectangular box—the corner fifty-four-inch mattress that had held us together through grief and eighty-nine-degree August heat, the unobstructed under-counter dorm fridge, the narrow natural-gas stove, the microwave cart pantry, and the yellow out-of-order tape waiting out in the lobby—neither Nolan nor I had to debate for half a heartbeat which side of the archway was home now.

Moving smoothly around our little room like a practiced two-person crew, we gathered the pieces of our Earth life we truly wanted with us. First, Nolan carefully lifted Grandpa Walter's silver-framed photograph off the oak dresser beside the rabbit-ear television and tucked it gently inside his padded laptop sleeve alongside his charcoal Quantum OS 2000 Professional laptop and power brick. I shut down my own upgraded Quantum OS 2000 Professional laptop cleanly—smiling at how zero crashes had paved the way to two real moons—slipped it and its cord into my shoulder bag, and draped Grandpa's plaid wool blanket over the backrest of my manual wheelchair by the dorm fridge.

Even with my front right caster fork still bent half an inch out of true, rolling both of our manual wheelchairs out of Studio 2B—with all four silver and charcoal Lofstrand forearm crutches strapped neatly behind our backrests—straight across the seamless, sill-free golden threshold back into the Uncharted Waystation felt like rolling out of a broom closet into a sunlit cathedral.

#### 7:45 PM — The Law of Two Pause-Held Worlds

![Inside Keepers' Hall in front of the Glowing Hearth-Archway and Vaeloria's twin moons, barefoot Nolan Lennox and his taller sister Gwen Lennox sit side by side in their two manual wheelchairs holding hands warmly across the smooth rose-quartz flagstones, with closed padded laptop bags, Grandpa Walter's plaid wool blanket, and Grandpa's silver-framed photograph angled toward Nolan's eyes on their laps while their four matte-silver and matte-charcoal single-tube Lofstrand forearm crutches rest cleanly beside the stone hearth and counter.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/e001/e001_c005_p002_wheelchairs_home_keepers_hall.jpg#fullwidth)

The instant our wheelchair tires glided out onto the warm rose-quartz flagstones of the Star-Court, I glanced up at Vaeloria's twilight sky—and gasped a second time.

Behind Archivist Thorne's shoulder, the exact curl of cedar hearth-smoke rising from Keepers' Hall hadn't drifted an inch higher while we spent fifteen minutes packing in Chesapeake, and out on the upper crag terrace Sister Caelia was still standing in the exact mid-wave gesture beside the bronze wind-harps!

Archivist Thorne smiled with gentle, knowing warmth at our astonished faces.

"You saw your home-clock standing still for you at nine-fifteen, didn't you?" the old scholar asked kindly. "That is the covenant the Slow-Footed Founders wove into the Waystation's rings so no Keeper would ever be torn in two by grief or haste. Time always stands still in whichever world you are not standing in. While the two of you walk beneath Vaeloria's twin moons, your room back on Earth waits unmoving on that exact breath of rain. And whenever the two of you step back to Earth—for an afternoon, a book, or a memory—every candle, hearth-pot, and star-ring here in Vaeloria pauses quietly until your wheels and crutches cross our threshold again."

Nolan looked from the smooth rose-quartz ramps of Keepers' Hall to me, hazel eyes bright with a peace I hadn't seen in him since we were little kids at Grandpa's table.

"Wait—we never lose either place," Nolan realized softly, reaching across the gap between our two wheelchairs to hold my hand tight. "Earth waits whenever we want to visit... and this glowing archway is really ours."

Within ten minutes, a cheerful Waystation ironsmith with a small brass truing wrench knelt beside my chair on the courtyard flagstones and straightened my bent front caster fork dead-level in four gentle turns—no forty-seven-minute speakerphone hold music, no six-week insurance denial letter, and zero charge beyond a shared mug of hot spiced cider.

That night inside Keepers' Hall, Nolan set Grandpa Walter's silver-framed photograph in the place of honor on the carved cedar shelf beside our wide Twin-Moon Canopy Bed, parked both of our smoothly rolling wheelchairs side by side near the warm stone hearth, and leaned our four Lofstrand forearm crutches cleanly into the carved armrest notches. Curled up close together under cloud-wool quilts and Grandpa Walter's green-and-navy plaid blanket while Vaeloria's twin moons shone clear through the Glowing Hearth-Archway of our home, we knew we could visit Studio 2B anytime we ever pleased—and we knew with both of our hearts right where we belonged forever.
