# Book One: Beyond the Living-Room Rift

## Chapter 1: No Wheelchairs in the Star-Moss

### Part 1: Amber Glass Over the Coffee Table

**Harmonic Cycle:** First Night of the Twin Moons, Borderland of Vaeloria • **Chronometer:** 21:15

![Under two glowing moons in a painted 1980s fantasy twilight meadow of soft silver-blue star-moss, barefoot 5-foot-7 stocky brother Nolan Lennox in a navy t-shirt and jeans and his taller 6-foot-1 curvy sister Gwen Lennox in a burgundy t-shirt and jeans sit hugging and cuddling closely together under their green-and-navy plaid sofa blanket on a mossy cedar log, with Nolan's matte-silver Lofstrand forearm crutches and Gwen's matte-charcoal Lofstrand forearm crutches leaning beside them.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/e001/e001_c001_p001_rift_meadow_arrival.jpg)

#### 9:15 PM — A Shimmer Above the Mug Ring

Rain ticked steady against our second-floor apartment window while the hold music from Regional Mobility Supply warbled through the speaker of Gwen's phone on the scratched oak coffee table for the forty-seventh straight minute.

Gwen reached over the edge of our green-and-navy plaid throw blanket, stabbed the red hang-up icon with her thumb, and let her head drop back against the saggy sofa cushion beside mine with a long groan. Even slouched down into the upholstery with her bare feet tucked up beside mine to stay off the drafty linoleum by the kitchen, my nineteen-year-old sister still had half a head of height on me. At six-foot-one to my five-seven, she was the one people always assumed was the older sibling until they heard me start nagging her to drink water.

"Closed for the weekend," Gwen said, tossing the phone onto the folded pile of prior-authorization denial letters. "Six weeks for one bent front caster fork on my chair, and the rep had the nerve to ask whether I really needed to leave the apartment before November."

"Good thing neither of us had glamorous Friday night plans," I said, pulling the plaid wool-blend blanket tighter across both our laps so her bare toes could steal heat off my shins the way she always did when the radiator clanked cold. Across the worn living-room rug near the front hall mat, both of our manual wheelchairs sat parked side by side—mine with a duct-taped left armrest pad, hers listing half an inch toward the bent right caster. Right against the padded arm of the sofa within easy reach leaned our four ergonomic Lofstrand forearm crutches: my shorter matte-silver pair on the outside, Gwen's taller matte-charcoal pair tucked inside them.

"Honestly?" Gwen murmured, tucking a thick wave of honey-brown hair behind her ear and resting her cheek against the crown of my head. "If there were a button on this coffee table that shipped the two of us somewhere without landlords, broken curb cuts, or medical billing codes, I wouldn't even stop to grab shoes."

"Careful," I teased. "Knowing our luck, the button would require three signatures from a—"

The air six inches above the water-ringed coffee table folded inward like warm amber stained glass.

There was no thunderclap and no smoke—just a deep, resonant hum like a brass cello string bowed inside a cathedral, and a sudden breeze smelling of crushed wild thyme, wet limestone, and night-blooming jasmine sweeping straight across our living room. Before I could even sit upright, the amber shimmer widened from the width of a dinner plate to the length of the entire sofa. The floor beneath the couch legs vanished into liquid gold light.

Instinct took over faster than thought. With my left arm I hooked all four aluminum forearm crutches clean off the armrest and clamped them hard against my heather-navy t-shirt; with my right hand I grabbed a fistful of the plaid blanket and Gwen's burgundy sleeve as the sofa cushion tilted out from under us.

"Nolan!" Gwen gasped, wrapping both arms around my shoulders.

We fell less than the height of a porch step.

Instead of slamming onto downstairs ceiling plaster, my bare heels and the seat of my worn blue jeans sank into three inches of cool, springy, velvet-soft moss that gave beneath our weight like a feathered quilt. Behind our backs, a faint oval of amber stained-glass light hung two feet above the turf—offering one fleeting glimpse of our empty living room rug and our two parked wheelchairs ten feet out of reach—before winking shut with a soft chime like a crystal goblet tapped by a fingernail.

Silence settled around us, vast and sweet and alive with distant crickets.

***

#### 9:40 PM — Two Moons and Zero Concrete

For ten seconds neither of us let go. Gwen's heart hammered fast against my shoulder through the plaid blanket; the four aluminum crutches rattled softly in my grip.

"Tell me I didn't hit my head on the coffee table," Gwen whispered.

"If you did, we're sharing the concussion," I breathed, looking up.

Above the silhouette of ancient, twisted silver-barked trees arched an indigo twilight sky that looked as if some master cinema painter from the nineteen-eighties had brushed it onto glass—deep sapphire melting into warm copper-gold along a horizon of towering, crystalline crags. Hanging side by side above the mountain saddle shone two moons: one large and pale ivory with mares shaped like a winged ship, the smaller one glowing soft rose-amber right off its shoulder. Every patch of moss around our bare ankles glimmered with tiny points of pale blue starlight whenever the night breeze stirred its fronds.

Gwen slowly uncurled her fingers from my shirt, pressed one bare sole flat into the glowing turf, and blinked in disbelief.

"Nolan—feel the ground," she said softly. "It's warm. Like moss sitting over a heated stone floor."

She was right. There wasn't a sharp pebble, sticker burr, or cold damp chill anywhere under our bare feet. Still, a quiet knot tightened in my stomach as I glanced around the empty glade where the amber oval had closed.

"Our chairs didn't come through," I said quietly, checking all four crutches across my knees—two silver, two charcoal, rubber tips intact, hinged forearm cuffs unbroken. "Just the blanket and the crutches."

I braced myself for the familiar wave of dread that would have hit either of us if we had been stranded ten blocks from home on concrete sidewalks without our wheelchairs.

Instead, Gwen slipped her forearms into her tall matte-charcoal cuffs, planted the rubber tips into the firm, cushiony turf, and pushed herself smoothly up beside a wide, mossy fallen cedar trunk—standing all six-foot-one in the twin moonlight while breathing in a lungful of air so clean and pine-sweet it almost made my eyes water. She reached one hand down to steady me as I clicked my silver cuffs around my forearms and eased up beside her to sit us both down on the smooth curve of the log, draping the plaid sofa blanket across our shoulders.

"Look around us, Nolan," Gwen said, her voice dropping to an awed hush as a pair of tiny, lantern-tailed moths drifted lazily past our bare toes. "Listen. No sirens. No upstairs neighbors stomping at midnight. No phones ringing. Just... air."

I wiggled my bare toes in the warm star-moss and looked down the gentle, silver-lit slope of the glade, where a ribbon of smooth pale flagstones wound out of the heather toward the distant glow of running water. My hips didn't ache from the landing. My shoulders felt lighter than they had in three years.

"Well," I said, nudging my shoulder up against hers under the plaid blanket, "you did say you wouldn't even stop to grab shoes."

Gwen let out a real, unguarded laugh—the brightest sound I had heard leave my sister's throat all year—and leaned her cheek against the top of my head as the twin moons climbed above the crags.

### Part 2: Six Inches Taller and Four Crutches Strong

**Harmonic Cycle:** Second Dawn of the Twin Moons, The Whispering Causeway • **Chronometer:** 07:40

![At golden-rose 1980s fantasy sunrise inside a sheltered mossy rose-quartz stone arch alcove beside a satin-smooth flagstone causeway and golden honey-plum orchard, barefoot 5-foot-7 stocky brother Nolan Lennox and his taller 6-foot-1 curvy sister Gwen Lennox wake curled up affectionately together on soft emerald moss under their green-and-navy plaid sofa blanket while all four of their silver and charcoal Lofstrand forearm crutches rest neatly against the smooth stone wall within arm's reach.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/e001/e001_c001_p002_ancient_causeway_dawn.jpg)

#### 7:40 AM — Satin Stone Under Bare Soles

Morning arrived in rose-gold and apricot across a sky so wide it made every ceiling I had ever lived under feel like a shoebox.

I woke warm and completely rested inside the dry, hollow lee of an ancient rose-quartz bridge abutment a hundred yards down from our arrival glade, still curled up closely against Nolan on a three-inch bed of velvety dry moss beneath our green-and-navy plaid sofa blanket. One of my arms was draped over my brother's broad shoulder, his tousled brown hair tucked right under my chin the way the two of us had always huddled together through winter apartment drafts and long hospital waiting-room nights. For three seconds my sleepy brain waited for the six-thirty garbage truck to rattle the alley dumpsters below our window. Instead, a chorus of liquid, flute-like birdcalls rippled from a grove of low orchard trees beside a clear tumbling rill, and warm golden sunlight spilled across our bare ankles without a trace of morning stiffness in my knees.

Nolan stirred against my chest, tightened his arm around my waist in a sleepy good-morning squeeze, and blinked lazily toward the sunlit arch where all four of our Lofstrand forearm crutches leaned side by side against the carved stone wall within easy reach.

"Morning, flagpole," my brother murmured against my collarbone before sitting up just enough to kiss the top of my hand and begin knotting two opposite corners of our plaid throw blanket into a neat diagonal shoulder sling so neither of us would lose a hand carrying it on crutches.

"Morning, shortstop," I smiled, giving his shoulders one last lingering hug before stretching my arms overhead and reaching for my charcoal pair. Nolan passed them to me grip-first the way we had handed each other mobility gear since we were kids. "Wait till I see this road you spotted last night—tell me my sleepy eyes aren't making up pink marble."

He wasn't exaggerating. Ten paces up a gentle grassy bank ran an ancient causeway twelve feet wide, paved from verge to verge in huge, hexagonal slabs of pale rose quartz fitted so tightly together a knife blade couldn't slip between the seams. Centuries of rain and wind had polished every stone to a warm, velvety satin—smooth enough that our bare soles glided over it without a single nick or scrape, yet textured just enough that the rubber ferrules of our four forearm crutches gripped solid on every swing-through step.

Better still, whoever had laid this old borderland road had clearly believed roads ought to let people sit down. Every two hundred paces along the low mossy parapet, the stonework flared outward into a curved, sun-warmed alcove bench set right beside small carved basins fed by cold, trickling mountain rills.

***

#### 10:15 AM — Honey-Plums and Zero Hurry

Back home, walking half a mile on crutches without our wheelchairs would have meant dodging cracked concrete heaves, waiting at four-lane intersections whose countdown timers flashed red before we were halfway across, and enduring at least two strangers asking what had happened to us before breakfast.

Out here on the Whispering Causeway, nobody timed us at all.

We set our own rhythm—swing, step, swing, step for ten minutes at an easy conversational pace while the morning sun warmed our cotton t-shirts, then five unhurried minutes sitting hip-to-hip on the next mossy stone alcove bench while our forearms rested and we watched iridescent blue dragonflies skim the roadside rill. At the third bench, an overarching grove of silver-leafed trees hung heavy with plump, golden-skinned fruit the size of peaches. We waited quietly until a flock of plump crested finches swooped in to peck greedily at three fallen ones—and after Nolan tested a tiny sliver on his tongue and waited twenty minutes just to be thorough, we bit into flesh that tasted like warm ripe pear swirled with wild clover honey.

Juice dripped off Nolan's chin onto the collar of his navy t-shirt; he wiped it with his forearm and looked back up the empty sunlit causeway toward the distant meadow where we had landed the night before.

"Gwen," he said quietly, turning a smooth honey-plum pit between his fingers. "Be honest with me. Are you panicked about finding a way back to the apartment?"

I looked down at my bare toes resting comfortably on warm rose quartz, then out across rolling emerald valleys framed by painted purple peaks and glittering waterfalls—a whole breathing world where the ground itself felt gentler than anything back home, and where my brother wasn't spending his twenties arguing on speakerphone with insurance adjusters while our supper went cold.

"Not even one percent," I admitted softly, resting my knee against his. "As long as the two of us stick together, Nolan... I want to see what's down this road."

Nolan's hazel eyes crinkled warm at the corners. He slipped the knotted plaid blanket sling across his chest, clicked his silver cuffs onto his forearms, and nodded down the gentle curve of the quartz causeway where a thin curl of sweet applewood hearth-smoke rose beside a distant stone bridge.

"Two hundred paces at a time," Nolan said. "Let's go see who's making cider."

## Chapter 2: The Three Roads of the Sunken Milestone

### Where the Flagstones Divide

**Harmonic Cycle:** Second Afternoon of the Twin Moons, The Sunken Milestone • **Chronometer:** 15:30

![In warm afternoon light at a mossy stone crossroads with a carved three-faced fantasy milestone where a sapphire-lantern canal dock, a lush emerald moss forest road, and a cliffside brass counterweight gondola diverge, barefoot 6-foot-1 Gwen Lennox wraps an arm affectionately around her shorter 5-foot-7 brother Nolan Lennox on a woven-rush cedar bench while they hold steaming mugs of spiced cider beside kindly white-whiskered peddler Bramwell Kettle and his lantern-hung cart, with all four of their silver and charcoal Lofstrand forearm crutches leaning cleanly beside them.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/e001/e001_c002_p001_three_roads_milestone.jpg)

#### 3:30 PM — Moonsilver Cuffs and Spiced Cider

The applewood smoke belonged to a round copper kettle bubbling on a folding iron brazier beside the cheerfulest wooden tinker's cart I had ever laid eyes on.

Strings of blown-glass lanterns in cobalt, amber, and sea-green clinked softly along its curved canvas ribs, while a gentle, broad-shouldered draught beast with curly russet wool and curled ram's horns munched clover in the shade of an ancient three-faced runestone milestone. Before Gwen and I had even finished the last twenty paces across the arched stone bridge on our crutches, a stout, white-whiskered man in a patched plum-velvet waistcoat and a soft wool cap hopped down off the cart's footboard with two earthenware mugs already steaming in his hands.

"Hail and easy step to you, travelers!" he called warmly, swinging down a wide cedar camp bench cushioned with woven river-rush before we even had to ask to sit. "Bramwell Kettle, licensed tinker of the Three Marches, at your service. Take the weight off your wrists and wrap your palms around some hot cinnamon-pear cider—this last league of the causeway climbs steeper than it looks!"

Neither his eyes nor his voice held a trace of pity, awkwardness, or intrusive curiosity as Gwen and I sank gratefully side by side onto the rush bench in our modern jeans and cotton t-shirts, leaning all four of our Lofstrand forearm crutches neatly against the wooden cart wheel. Gwen immediately looped her left arm affectionately around my shoulders, tucking me close against her side while our bare feet rested in cool clover. If anything, Bramwell squinted with pure craftsman's admiration at the brushed matte-silver aluminum of my single-shaft forearm tubes.

"Now that is fine tube-smithing," Bramwell marveled respectfully, not touching them without permission. "Hollow-drawn moonsilver alloy, by the lightness of your swing! Most borderland folk still carve ashwood walking-staves ten times heavier."

"Lightweight alloy from where we grew up," Gwen said with a smile, warming both hands around her earthenware mug while her bare feet rested in soft clover. "Though we lost our wheeled chairs on the way in, so we're figuring out where this causeway goes at crutch speed."

"Ah! Then you could not have stopped at a luckier stone in all Vaeloria," Bramwell beamed, gesturing with his ladle toward the tall, moss-crowned three-faced milestone standing at the center of the plaza.

***

#### 4:15 PM — Three Gentle Ways Deeper Into the Realm

From the circular flagstone apron beyond the milestone, the single borderland causeway split cleanly into three completely different ways forward—each winding toward the misty heart of the realm beneath towering painted clouds.

To our left, stone steps shallow as ramp-slabs descended to a tranquil, mirror-clear waterway lined with woven reed docks: the Glasswater Canal. Tied beside the cedar bollards floated a wide, flat-bottomed timber vessel forty feet long, its carved prow hung with glowing sapphire marsh-lanterns and its open deck cushioned with thick rush mats while a calm woman in a river-cloak coiled hemp rope at the stern.

"That there is Maren's route along the Glasswater Canal," Bramwell explained, pointing east. "Smooth current does ninety percent of the walking for you. Travelers sit cushions-up with a warm stewpot on the cabin stove, watching the reed-villages and star-heron marshes glide past while Maren poles from lock to lock."

Straight ahead between two carved stone owl pillars, the flagstones vanished beneath a living carpet of thick, luminous emerald turf winding into a cathedral-tall forest of silver-barked rowans and giant, softly glowing amber-and-azure canopy blossoms.

"Through the middle arches lies the Whisperwood Moss-Road," Bramwell continued fondly. "Laid down an age ago by the Slow-Footed Order, who vowed never to build a road that bruised an unshod heel. Three inches of springy cushion-moss the whole way in, stone rest-alcoves at every quarter-mile, and warm crystal mineral springs bubbling right beside the trail whenever your joints want a hot soak."

Then he pointed upward to our right, where tiers of rose-gold limestone cliffs rose into the painted afternoon clouds—and gliding smoothly up an angled span of braided bronze cables between stone towers rode an open, spacious gondola car of polished oak and gleaming brass counterweighted by slow water-wheels.

"And up the right-hand terrace runs the Brass Counterweight Way into the Amber Crags," Bramwell said, eyes twinkling. "Not a single stair to climb—the water-balance lifts carry you smooth as butter from terrace to terrace past cliffside wind-chimes and the star-keepers' warm abbey kitchens, where they map the twin moons on rings of brass."

Bramwell refilled both of our mugs with a splash of hot spiced cider and tipped his wool cap. "All three routes wind inward toward the high heart of the realm in their own good time, friends, and none of them asks a traveler to march like a foot-soldier. You only have to pick which kind of road suits the two of you best."

Gwen looked from the sapphire lanterns bobbing gently on the canal boat, to the lush emerald moss road glowing beneath the forest arch, to the smooth brass gondola rising into the golden crags—then looked down at me with shining green-hazel eyes that didn't miss our old apartment one bit.

"No wrong doors, brother," Gwen said softly. "Which way do we go first?"

***

## Chapter 3: Lanterns on the Glasswater

### Drifting Under Sapphire Glass

**Harmonic Cycle:** Second Twilight of the Twin Moons, The Glasswater Canal • **Chronometer:** 18:45

![At cobalt-and-gold 1980s fantasy twilight along the mirror-calm Glasswater Canal, barefoot 5-foot-7 Nolan Lennox and his taller 6-foot-1 sister Gwen Lennox cuddle warmly together under their green-and-navy plaid sofa blanket on the cushioned prow bench of a wide cedar barge hung with sapphire and amber glass lanterns, Gwen resting her head on Nolan's shoulder while their silver and charcoal single-tube Lofstrand forearm crutches rest cleanly against the wooden gunwales.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/paths/route1_lantern_barge_canal.jpg)

#### 6:45 PM — Zero Weight on Our Knees

The wide cedar gangplank bridging the stone quay to Maren's vessel had no cleats to trip bare toes and sloped gentler than any ramp back home.

"Take the forward rush-bench right behind the prow lantern," Maren said in a low, unhurried contralto, steadying the tiller bar with one weathered hand while waiting until both Nolan and I were seated side by side before she cast off the stern line. "Everything aboard floats level—no pitching, no rushing. Stow your moonsilver staves right in the padded rack along the rail."

The moment I unclipped my charcoal cuffs, laid my crutches beside Nolan's silver pair in the felt-lined gunwale cradle, and stretched both legs out across the thick woven cattail cushions with our plaid throw blanket tucked over our laps, a sigh went through my whole spine from neck to heel.

With one smooth push of Maren's ashwood pole against the mossy canal wall, the forty-foot reed-and-cedar vessel glided out into the deep, mirror-still ribbon of the Glasswater Canal.

There was no engine roar and no diesel exhaust—only the soft liquid *hush* of water parting beneath the rounded prow and the warm glow of six blown-glass sapphire and amber lanterns swaying gently from carved wooden hooks overhead. Along both banks, cottages roofed in golden thatch lit their evening hearth-candles behind round leaded windows, and children waving willow-wand sparklers called friendly good-evenings across the water without a single person staring twice at two barefoot travelers in denim jeans.

***

#### 8:20 PM — Navigating by Double Moonlight

When full indigo twilight deepened between the whispering reed beds and both moons rose clear in the water ahead of our bow, Maren brought forward two bowls of barley-and-root stew fragrant with wild rosemary and set a small brass sight-compass down on the low chest between us.

"My eyes aren't as sharp for the faint marsh-stars as they were twenty winters back," Maren admitted warmly, nodding toward the shimmering twin-moon reflections stretching down the canal toward a fork of distant stone water-arches. "If either of you has an eye for star-angles above the mist, you'll more than earn your supper and cabin bunks all the way upriver."

Nolan grinned through a spoonful of hot stew and nudged my elbow. Back home, I had spent half my sleepless nights reading astronomy charts on my phone when leg cramps kept me awake; nobody had ever called it useful.

I lifted the warm brass sight-ring to my eye, lined its hairline wire between the smaller rose-amber moon and a cluster of seven blue-white stars hanging like a lantern hook above the eastern reeds, and traced their mirror-twin angle across the black-glass water.

"Two points north of the willow island," I called softly toward the stern. "The deep channel runs straight under the middle arch where the silver-star reflection stays unbroken."

Maren leaned on the tiller, felt the deep, silent pull of the main current catch our keel clean as silk, and let out an approving whistle. "Sharpest night-sight on the canal all season, tall sister! You two have a berth aboard this boat as far upriver as you care to roam."

Nolan leaned back against the cushioned prow beside me under our shared plaid blanket, wiggling his bare toes in the warm lantern glow while silvery night-herons glided soundlessly alongside our gunwale.

"Nobody on hold," Nolan murmured against my arm. "Nobody telling us we're in the way. And we're moving ten miles tonight without taking a single painful step."

I rested my head down on my brother's shoulder, watching two moons ripple in blue-and-gold ribbons ahead of our bow, already certain in my bones that whatever waited at the far end of these waterways was going to become our real home.

## Chapter 3: The Slow-Footed Order's Road

### Warm Springs and Glowing Canopy

**Harmonic Cycle:** Second Twilight of the Twin Moons, The Whisperwood • **Chronometer:** 18:30

![Beneath towering silver-barked rowan trees and softly glowing blue-and-gold bioluminescent canopy blossoms in a 1980s fantasy forest, barefoot 5-foot-7 Nolan Lennox and his taller 6-foot-1 sister Gwen Lennox sit hugging affectionately side by side on a mossy carved stone bench with their jeans cuffs rolled up, soaking their bare feet in a gently steaming crystal-clear warm mineral spring while their silver and charcoal Lofstrand forearm crutches lean cleanly beside them.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/paths/route2_whisperwood_moss_road.jpg)

#### 6:30 PM — Built for Unhurried Feet

Fifty yards past the carved stone owl pillars, I realized Bramwell Kettle hadn't been spinning a tall tale about the Slow-Footed Order.

Every public trail Gwen and I had ever tried to visit back on Earth had been designed for brisk hikers in lug-soled boots—loose crushed gravel that skidded out from under crutch tips, exposed tree roots waiting to catch a toe, or steep timber water-bars with nowhere to sit for two miles. Stepping onto the Whisperwood Moss-Road felt like walking across a royal carpet woven specifically for the two of us.

Beneath a vaulting archway of ancient silver-barked rowan trees grew an unbroken twelve-foot-wide ribbon of dense emerald cushion-moss three inches thick, springy enough to absorb half the shock through our wrists and shoulders on every crutch swing yet firm enough that our rubber ferrules never sank or snagged. Better still, as afternoon slanted into soft forest dusk, thousands of bell-shaped blossoms high in the bioluminescent canopy above our heads began to open—bathing the entire trail in warm amber and tranquil sea-blue lantern light so there were zero dark shadows hiding the ground ahead.

"Nolan, look at the mile-markers," Gwen said, pausing beside a waist-high carved stone post shaped like a curled sleeping badger. Cut into its flat top in clean, raised lettering—astonishingly readable—sat a quiet inscription worn smooth by centuries of grateful palms:

*No league is measured here by haste. Sit when the breath asks; walk when the song returns.*

***

#### 7:50 PM — Ninety-Eight Degrees of Liquid Velvet

At the third quarter-mile alcove, the sweet, clean scent of warm mountain minerals drifted through the fern fronds before we even rounded the curve.

Tucked beside a dry-roofed stone pilgrim shelter stocked neatly with split cedar kindling, an iron roasting pan, and a woven basket of sweet forest chestnuts sat a circular basin of smooth white travertine stone fed by an underground thermal spring. Wisps of gentle steam curled off crystal-clear water into the blue-and-gold forest twilight.

Gwen and I didn't even have to vote. We rolled the cuffs of our blue jeans halfway up our calves, propped all four forearm crutches side by side in the carved stone notches built right into the bench arm—some ancient mason had literally chiseled walking-staff rests into every bench!—and lowered our bare feet together into the shallow travertine rim.

Both of us let out the exact same involuntary sound of pure bliss.

The mineral water was ninety-eight degrees to the drop, buoyant and silky around tired ankles, arches, and calves without a trace of sulfur smell. Within two minutes every tight tendon in my shins and forearms unknotted completely, while a tiny red-breasted forest robin hopped fearlessly onto the opposite rim to take a sip before ruffling its feathers at us.

"Back home," Gwen said softly, leaning her tall shoulder down against mine while wiggling her toes in the warm crystal ripples, "physical therapy charged ninety dollars a copay for fifteen minutes in a stainless-steel whirlpool tub under buzzing fluorescent tubes."

"And nobody handed out free chestnuts afterward," I agreed, reaching over to set a handful of sweet scored chestnuts onto the flat warming-stone beside the shelter's small hearth-brazier until their shells popped fragrant and golden.

As the canopy blossoms brightened overhead like an indoor galaxy of blue and gold stars and the warm water cradled our bare feet, neither Gwen nor I looked back toward the borderland bridge once. Whatever lay deeper along this mossy road, this forest already treated us more like family than the world we had left behind.

## Chapter 3: Above the Painted Clouds

### Gondolas of the Amber Crags

**Harmonic Cycle:** Second Sunset of the Twin Moons, The Amber Crags • **Chronometer:** 18:20

#### 6:20 PM — Water-Wheels and Singing Brass

Every broken subway elevator and locked "out-of-order" wheelchair lift Nolan and I had ever stared at in the rain got its cosmic apology the moment we rolled through the lower pavilion of the Brass Counterweight Way.

There were no stairs anywhere in the rose-limestone station—only broad, sweeping flagstone ramps polished smooth as butter leading straight flush to the open double doors of a wide, boat-hull-shaped counterweight gondola crafted from seasoned golden oak and filigreed tarnish-free brass. A young cliff-attendant in an russet wool tabard named Pippin didn't ask for a permit or a doctor's note; he simply latched the brass gate snug once Nolan and I settled side by side onto the deep, wool-cushioned bench, tucked our four forearm crutches safely into leather loop-straps beside our knees, and pulled a smooth wooden water-valve lever on the landing.

Deep beneath the stone floor, mountain snowmelt poured silently into a twin oak ballast tank.

Without a lurch or rattle, our gondola lifted smoothly along four braided bronze sky-cables, rising out of the valley shadow straight into the blazing gold-and-violet sunset of the Amber Crags.

"Okay," Nolan breathed, wrapping our green-and-navy plaid throw blanket snug around both our shoulders as a warm updraft smelled of high mountain cedar and sun-baked stone. "That beats the third-floor apartment elevator by about ten thousand miles."

All around us, towering spires of rose and amber stone caught the painted nineteen-eighties sunset light, and bolted into the natural wind-arches of the cliff face stood tall, curved frames strung with ribbons of tuned phosphor-bronze: ancient wind-harps that hummed a slow, resonant major chord whenever the evening breeze glided up from the valley below.

#### 7:35 PM — The Star-Keepers' Upper Terrace

Twelve minutes of effortless sky-gliding delivered our car dead-level onto the flagstone veranda of the First Crag Observatory, where round copper lanterns glowed beside an open stone kitchen smelling gloriously of baked herb flatbread and hot honeyed milk.

Waiting on the terrace beside a six-foot armillary sphere of nested brass rings stood Sister Caelia, an elderly astronomer with silver braids and ink-stained cuffs who greeted our bare feet and cotton t-shirts as warmly as if we were visiting scholars arriving by royal carriage. While Nolan accepted a warm wooden platter of rosemary flatbread straight off the hearthstone so we could eat sitting down on a cushioned stone window-seat overlooking fifty leagues of twilight cloud-sea, my eyes went straight to a stuck vernier gear on Caelia's outer moon-ring.

"The rose moon runs three degrees ahead of the ivory moon every fourth night, doesn't it?" I asked, tilting my head as the twin moons cleared the eastern crags. "Your outer brass ring is pinned for a circular orbit, not an elliptical sway."

Sister Caelia froze with her brass dividers mid-air, stared from the twin moons to my finger pointing at the third vernier pin, and broke into a radiant grin.

"Child, three apprentices have argued with that gear since midsummer without seeing the ellipse!" she exclaimed. "If you and your brother are traveling inward across the crag-spans, you have honored guest quarters at every sky-terrace between here and the high peaks—no climbs, warm hearths, and all the star-charts you care to read!"

Nolan tore a steaming piece of herb flatbread in half, passed me the bigger piece, and clinked his mug of warm honeyed milk softly against mine as the cliffside wind-harps sang out across the violet clouds.

"Hear that, flagpole?" Nolan grinned, resting his bare feet beside mine on a thick woven hearth-rug. "We've been in this world less than twenty-four hours and you're already fixing ancient astronomical instruments."

Looking out past the brass rings toward endless painted horizons where nobody treated crutches like an inconvenience or height like an oddity, I squeezed my brother's hand under our old sofa blanket—already knowing in my heart that Earth could keep our apartment keys forever.
