# Book 3: The NCLEX

## Chapter 1: The Licensure Exam

### Part 1: The Scope of Practice

**Date:** 2005-01-10 at 19:00 PT

The living room of Cassandra's Federal Way apartment was quiet, save for the rhythmic tapping of my keyboard and the soft hum of the rain against the glass sliding door. 

Winter Quarter at Northwest Pacific University had officially started. Liz and I were completely settled into our academic routines, taking the express bus from Federal Way directly to the NPU campus in Seattle, while Cassandra navigated her own commute toward the massive Quantum corporate campus. 

The logistics were flawless. We were comfortably anchored in the Federal Way apartment while Cassandra finalized the staggering corporate paperwork required to purchase our permanent house out in Redmond. The perimeter was entirely secure. But as I sat at the kitchen island with my laptop open, staring at the Washington State Department of Health's regulatory guidelines, I realized we had a massive, looming vulnerability.

For years, I had always assumed that just being Cassandra's brother—and being physically capable of assisting her with her Activities of Daily Living (ADLs)—would be enough to act as her primary caregiver once we were out from under our parents' roof. 

I was wrong. 

I clicked through the heavy, legalistic PDFs outlining the state's scope of practice for home care. Cassandra didn't just need someone to help push her manual wheelchair or reach high shelves. She had complex, underlying neurological needs stemming from her cerebral palsy. She required highly specific, regulated medication management, complex physical transfers, and the constant monitoring of autonomic functions that could rapidly destabilize. 

If she ever needed a specialized intervention, or if we ever needed to interface with her neurologists or secure institutional accommodations, a family member with no medical background had zero legal authority to execute clinical care. I would be legally barred from performing anything beyond basic, non-medical assistance. We would be entirely dependent on third-party home health agencies—strangers coming into our house, breaching our secure perimeter, and dictating her daily schedule. 

Cassandra would absolutely hate that. It would destroy her autonomy. 

I opened a new tab on OmniSearch and began cross-referencing the credentialing tiers. 

A Certified Nursing Assistant (CNA) was the absolute baseline, but the scope of practice was far too narrow. A CNA couldn't legally administer medications, manage complex therapies, or handle acute physiological shifts. It was primarily focused on basic bathing, feeding, and mobility. It wasn't enough. 

A Registered Nurse (RN) was the gold standard, but it required a brutal, multi-year bachelor's degree that would completely derail my current academic trajectory and delay my ability to step in as her primary provider. 

And then, right in the middle of the credentialing matrix, I found the exact puzzle piece I needed: the Licensed Practical Nurse (LPN). 

The LPN scope of practice was incredibly robust. It authorized the administration of medications, the execution of complex nursing plans, the management of specialized medical equipment, and the legal ability to interface directly with physicians on a clinical level. It was the exact level of clearance required to legally, safely, and autonomously manage Cassandra's care without ever needing to rely on a third-party agency. 

I highlighted the LPN requirements on the screen, my heart accelerating. 

It wasn't an easy credential. It required intense anatomy and physiology prerequisites, hundreds of hours of clinical rotations, and passing the grueling NCLEX-PN national board exam. 

But as I pulled up the course catalog for Northwest Pacific University's College of Nursing and started mapping out the prerequisite tracks for their LPN certification program, a massive dual-track academic path suddenly crystallized. 

I wasn't going to drop my tech major—I still loved tinkering with HTML and building out local networks. But I was going to run the LPN curriculum right alongside it. I didn't plan on being a full-time career nurse. I just needed the clinical clearance. 

I was going to earn my LPN, so that the next time I sat in a room with Cassandra's primary care physicians, I wouldn't just be the "helpful brother." I would be speaking to them as a fellow medical professional, and I was going to permanently secure my sister's autonomy.

## Chapter 2: The Invasion

### Part 1: The Sea-Tac Crush-Load

"They're coming, Case," Liz laughed, squeezing my waist.

### Part 2: The Route 194 Deployment

**Date:** 2005-03-18 at 14:45 PT

The lower transit drive at Sea-Tac Airport was bustling with travelers, but our pack completely dominated the sidewalk. Moving fourteen people and a small mountain of luggage required military precision, but the East Coast household was used to operating as a single, highly coordinated unit. 

The teal, gold, and white TAPS King County Route 194 bus pulled up to the curb with a heavy hiss of air brakes. The front doors folded open, but the operator didn't immediately lower the hydraulic ramp. Instead, his eyes widened as he looked out at our massive group and the three wheelchairs waiting on the sidewalk. 

He leaned out of his seat, looking down at us apologetically. "I'm sorry, folks. I can take your group, but I only have two wheelchair tie-down bays on this bus. It's a federal safety regulation. I can't have a third chair blocking the aisle. You'll have to split up and wait for the next bus."

Cassandra rolled forward, positioning herself right at the edge of the curb near the open doors. 

"I am entirely aware of the TAPS operational guidelines," Cassandra stated smoothly, projecting her voice so the operator could hear her clearly over the idling engine. "I will be executing a physical transfer into a standard priority seat, and my brother will fold and stow my manual chair frame in the aisle. The two tie-down bays will be exclusively utilized by the other two chairs. We are not splitting up."

The operator blinked, processing the logistical solution, then offered a relieved nod. "Alright, if you can transfer and fold it, come on aboard."

He hit the pneumatic switch, and the heavy hydraulic ramp finally folded out onto the concrete. 

Cassandra rolled up the ramp first, immediately maneuvering her manual chair next to the priority seating at the front of the bus. She locked her brakes, planted her feet, and executed a flawless, perfectly controlled physical transfer into the closest aisle seat. 

I stepped onto the bus right behind her, dropping my backpack. Within five seconds, I popped the quick-release wheels off Cassandra's manual chair, folded the ultra-light titanium frame in half, and tucked the entire assembly securely between my legs as I sat down next to her. 

The operator blinked, clearly impressed by the sheer speed of the maneuver. "Well, that works. Fares?"

Cassandra reached into her coat and dropped the heavy rolls of quarters directly into the farebox hopper. The machine loudly chugged as it processed the exact change for fourteen adults. 

"I will also need fourteen paper transfer slips, please," Cassandra requested formally. 

The operator nodded, tearing off a thick stack of paper transfers and handing them to her as Kate and David Delaney successfully rolled up the ramp and secured themselves into the two designated tie-down bays. 

The rest of the pack swarmed the bus, dragging suitcases down the aisle and taking up nearly every available seat in the front half of the vehicle. Casey sat across from me, bouncing her leg with excitement, while Meredith and Morgan took the seats directly behind us, loudly arguing about the Seattle weather. 

"So, where exactly are we getting off?" Liz asked, holding onto the grab rail as the bus merged onto Interstate 5, speeding north toward the city skyline. 

"Westlake Station," Cassandra answered, handing the stack of paper transfers to Megan to distribute. "Route 194 utilizes the Downtown Seattle Transit Tunnel. It is a fully grade-separated subterranean route, which bypasses all surface traffic."

"Why Westlake?" Erin asked from a few rows back. "I looked at a map on the plane. Pioneer Square is closer to the waterfront."

"Because Pioneer Square is built on a massive, unforgiving hill," I explained, leaning back in my seat. "You try pushing a mountain of luggage up a forty-five-degree incline while navigating historic cobblestones. Westlake is in the retail core. It’s significantly flatter."

"David is correct," Cassandra nodded. "The Cascade Crest Hotel is entirely accessible from the Westlake Station surface elevators. It requires exactly two blocks of flat, paved travel. We do not do hills unless operationally necessary."

The bus plunged into the Downtown Seattle Transit Tunnel, the roar of the diesel engine shifting as the dual-mode systems adjusted. Since it was March 2005, the tunnel was still fully open to bus traffic, completely insulated from the heavy rain that had just started to fall on the streets above. 

When the automated voice announced Westlake Station, our massive pack mobilized. I quickly reassembled Cassandra's chair in the aisle, allowing her to transfer seamlessly back into her seat. As we filed off the bus and onto the subterranean platform, I noticed a series of bright yellow transit placards plastered across the tiled walls, warning riders that the entire tunnel would be shutting down in September for a massive, two-year light rail track retrofit.

We deployed across the platform in a highly organized wave. We commandeered the station elevator, taking two trips to get all fourteen people and three wheelchairs up to the surface. 

When the elevator doors opened onto Pine Street, the cool, damp Pacific Northwest air hit us instantly. But looking at the massive, tight-knit group of people surrounding me on the sidewalk, the Seattle gloom didn't matter at all. The perimeter was locked, the pack was home, and Spring Break had officially begun.

### Part 3: Checking In

**Date:** 2005-03-18 at 15:20 PT

The walk from Westlake Station to the Cascade Crest Hotel was a short, flat, perfectly paved two-block commute. After enduring the chaos of the airport and the subterranean rumble of the transit tunnel, the massive glass doors of the hotel lobby offered a luxurious, quiet sanctuary. 

We swarmed the lobby. Moving fourteen people into any space instantly changed its dynamic, and the East Coast pack immediately claimed a large section of the plush leather couches near the front windows. Suitcases and duffel bags were piled into a defensive perimeter. 

Cassandra rolled directly up to the front desk, the heavy marble counter sitting at eye level for her. I stood right behind her right shoulder, offering a silent physical escort. 

"Good afternoon, I am checking in under Vance," Cassandra announced to the front desk agent. "It is a large family block reservation. We are expected."

The agent tapped on her keyboard, her eyes widening slightly as she pulled up the massive reservation. "Yes, Ms. Vance. Seven suites on the twelfth floor. The reservations were made months ago, and since it's just past three o'clock, all of your rooms are fully serviced and ready for entry."

"Excellent," Cassandra said, reaching into her suit jacket. 

She absolutely despised using credit cards. Cassandra was a strict, meticulous accountant who vastly preferred the immediate transaction settlement of a debit card. Debt of any kind, even temporary rolling debt, bothered her. But she also understood hotel logistics. Hotels despised debit cards for incidentals and holds. So, playing the game, she pulled out a sleek, heavy personal credit card and handed it over. 

"I am placing the entire block on this card," Cassandra instructed firmly. "That includes all room charges, taxes, and a hundred percent of all incidentals. Any room service, any meals charged to the suites by anyone in my party—it all routes to this master account."

Cassandra didn't even turn her head. She just held her hand up in a gentle, but utterly immovable stop gesture. 

"Wendy, you flew across the continent to support my brother," Cassandra said, her tone carrying the absolute, uncompromising weight of a Quantum executive. "You provided him with a sanctuary in Virginia Beach when he needed it most. You are my guests. Your money is no good in the Pacific Northwest."

"It's easily thousands of dollars, Cassandra," Tom warned, stepping up beside his wife. 

"It is a rounding error," Cassandra corrected him without a trace of arrogance. It was just a logistical fact. "The hotel is paid for. The meals are paid for. You are here to enjoy Spring Break with my family."

Wendy looked at me, completely stunned by the sheer financial force of my sister.

Cassandra, Kate, and David Delaney simply rolled into the empty bays, effortlessly docking their wheelchairs directly up to the table without needing to execute a single awkward transfer or ask for furniture to be rearranged. The engine was finally running at full power.

### Part 7: Interlude: The Bellevue Bidding War

The ink on the massive real estate contract was still wet.

Cassandra Vance had executed a flawless, overwhelming preemptive strike. Her all-cash bid, backed by her vested Quantum stock options, was completely unprecedented. Then his cell phone rang. 

Then his assistant’s phone rang.

## Chapter 8: The Prospector's Keep

### Part 1: The BRAT Diet

**Date:** 2005-03-23 at 09:00 PT

"I'm starving," Casey murmured, her voice thick with sleep. 

"Me too," I groaned, trying to sit up. 

As soon as my abdominal muscles contracted, a wave of deep, physical soreness radiated across my stomach. The nausea was completely gone, but the violent, repeated muscular contractions of yesterday's purge had battered the organ wall and the surrounding abdominal muscles. "BRAT?" I asked, taking a tiny, cautious sip of the peppermint tea. The warmth instantly felt soothing against my battered throat.

I wouldn't have traded it for anything.
