# Book 1: The George Wythe Expedition

## Chapter 1: The Northern Regional – Summer 1995

### Part 1: Departing NPN – 8:00 AM

# Part 1: Departing NPN – 8:00 AM

The humid morning air of Virginia Beach was already clinging to us by the time my father parked the family station wagon at the Newport News (NPN) transit station. It was the summer of 1995, and I was ten years old. My little sister, Casey, was eight, and she was currently vibrating with the uncontainable energy of a child who firmly believed she was about to make a massive, life-altering decision.

"I just need to see the campus," Casey insisted, marching beside my father as he pulled our rolling suitcases toward the rail platform. "I know VDU is closer, and their summer camp is great, but George Wythe has a completely different architectural profile. I need to physically walk the quad."

My mother smiled tiredly, handing me my backpack. "You are eight, Casey. You have a decade before you have to declare a major."

"It is never too early to optimize," Casey shot back, adjusting the straps of her brightly colored backpack. She was right about the summer camps, though; as soon as we got back from this open house trip, both of us were being shipped off. I had been going for a few years now, but this was going to be Casey's first time at the Virginia Dominion University summer session, and she was treating it like a serious academic fellowship.

Rolling effortlessly alongside us was my older sister, Cassandra. At seventeen, Cassandra was a rising senior at Northwood High School, and she was the actual reason we were all standing on a train platform before eight in the morning.

Cassandra was navigating her manual wheelchair with the kind of casual, practiced fluid motion that came from years of muscle memory. Her axial load injury was just a standard fact of our family's logistics now, an unquestioned baseline that Cassandra had brutally optimized long ago. 

"The ARTS Northeast Regional is on time," Cassandra announced, glancing down at a printed itinerary resting on her lap. She didn't look up as she adjusted her glasses. "We connect in Alexandria. From there, we transition to the Capital Transit bus network to Dunn Loring, catch the Orange Line, and then intercept the CCT at Vienna. It is a seamless loop."

My father sighed, looking at the massive, folding accessibility map my sister had meticulously highlighted. "Cassandra, I'm all for taking the train up to ALX. We always do. But there is absolutely no reason to subject the entire family to a surface bus transfer through Seven Corners. We could just get on the Blue Line, ride it to Rosslyn, and transfer directly to the Orange Line to Vienna."

"The Rosslyn elevator bank is a highly volatile variable," Cassandra replied instantly, her voice carrying that sharp, unyielding certainty that she used whenever someone questioned her logistics. "A hydraulic failure at a deep-level transfer station would completely sever our route. Furthermore, I need to verify the accessibility tolerances of the Capital Transit surface grid. If I am going to accept an offer from George Wythe University, I must know that I can navigate the metropolitan area autonomously even when the rail network degrades. I will not be tethered to an elevator shaft."

"You're making us ride a bus through Seven Corners," my mother muttered, rubbing her temples. Having grown up right along that stretch of US-50, she knew the geography intimately. "I hated driving through that intersection as a teenager, Cassandra. It is a chaotic nightmare on a good day."

"The CCT and Capital Transit drivers are professionals," Cassandra countered, finally locking her brakes as we reached the edge of the platform. "And the route is mathematically sound."

I looked at Cassandra. Even at seventeen, she didn't just plan trips; she executed operations. She was already treating her college tours like tactical deployments. Once this George Wythe trip was over, and Casey and I finished our VDU summer camps, the family was flying all the way to Washington State to tour the Seattle campus of Northwest Pacific University.

But for now, the distant, heavy blast of a train horn echoed down the tracks. The ARTS Northeast Regional was pulling into NPN. Cassandra unlocked her brakes, her eyes fixed sharply on the horizon. The great college expedition had officially begun.

### Part 2: The Northern Regional – 8:30 AM

# Part 2: The Northern Regional – 8:30 AM

The ARTS Northeast Regional train rocked gently as it cleared the limits of Newport News, settling into a smooth, high-speed cadence on the rails heading north. 

Inside the designated accessibility car, our seating arrangement was mathematically precise. Cassandra was parked securely in the open wheelchair bay, her brakes locked. Because of the orientation of the tie-downs, she was facing backward relative to the train’s movement, allowing her to look directly at Casey and me. 

My sister and I were seated side-by-side in the two fold-down companion seats facing Cassandra. Just behind us, Mom and Dad were settled into a standard pair of forward-facing seats, within earshot but thankfully out of the immediate blast radius of Casey's academic interrogation. 

"I have already analyzed the George Wythe University computer science curriculum," Casey announced, crossing her eight-year-old arms over her chest and swinging her legs. Her feet didn’t even touch the floor of the train. "It is a highly competitive program, Cassandra. But we need to look at the macro-level logistics. VDU is practically in our backyard. The Virginia Dominion campus has excellent STEM pathways. I should know, I've toured it thoroughly."

"You attended a VDU summer camp orientation," Cassandra corrected mildly, pulling a thick spiral-bound college course catalog from her messenger bag and dropping it onto her lap. "That is not a collegiate tour, Casey."

"It gave me a baseline understanding of the architecture," Casey argued without missing a beat. "And if we go to Seattle to tour NPU later this summer, you'll be dealing with severe topographical elevation changes on the Seattle campus. Washington State is not flat. Have you calculated the gradient of the walkways at Northwest Pacific?"

I leaned my head back against the window, watching the blur of the Virginia treeline. I was ten, and I was mostly just excited we were taking the train instead of driving, but Casey was acting like she was a senior admissions counselor personally tasked with evaluating Cassandra’s future. 

"The NPU campus gradients have already been factored into my mobility profile," Cassandra replied seamlessly, completely indulging our eight-year-old sister's intense line of questioning. "But George Wythe offers a distinct advantage regarding proximity to the District's tech corridor. The Dulles technology hub is expanding. A computer science degree from GWU provides immediate lateral access to federal contracting and emerging tech sectors."

"But what about the dorms?" Casey pressed, leaning forward. "We have to inspect the dorms today. You can't just look at the academic buildings. We need to verify the turning radius in the bathrooms and the desk heights."

"We will," Cassandra promised, tapping the course catalog. "We have an appointment with their disability counselor this afternoon. I intend to inspect a standard freshman housing unit."

From the seats behind us, my father leaned forward, resting his forearms on the back of my chair. "I still don't understand why we couldn't just book a room at a chain hotel in Rosslyn. Why are we staying at a motel in Vienna?"

Cassandra didn't even blink. "Because the motel in Vienna is located directly on the CCT bus loop. The CCT acts as a dedicated transit tether between the Vienna Orange Line station and the George Wythe campus. If I live on campus, I will need to use that loop constantly. Therefore, we must establish a base camp along the route to test its operational frequency."

"And she booked us two adjoining rooms," Casey added proudly, as if she had personally negotiated the hotel contract. "Mom and Dad in one room, and the three of us in the accessible room. With a connecting door."

Mom let out a soft laugh from the row behind us. "Yes, thank you, Casey. We are well aware of the rooming logistics. Just remember that tomorrow morning is going to be an early start."

"I know," Casey said, her expression turning deadly serious. "The Open House continental breakfast begins at exactly 0730 hours. I have to secure a good table before the welcoming speech."

I looked out the window again, smiling. Casey wasn't just tagging along on her older sister's college tour. In her mind, she was already enrolled.

### Part 3: The Seven Corners Transfer – 12:45 PM

# Part 3: The Seven Corners Transfer – 12:45 PM

The moment the ARTS Northeast Regional pulled into Alexandria Station (ALX), our family’s transition from passengers to a unified logistical unit was immediate.

"Deploy the ramp," Cassandra commanded quietly to the conductor as the doors slid open. Once her wheels hit the concrete, she took point. 

My father grabbed our two largest rolling suitcases, while Mom handled the garment bags. Casey—wearing her brightly colored backpack and clutching a printed Capital Transit schedule as if it were a classified dossier—marched right beside Cassandra. I trailed just behind them, pulling my own small suitcase.

"We need to clear the platform," Casey instructed, her eight-year-old voice trying to project authority over the din of the station. "The long ramp is ahead, which leads to the subterranean tunnel. That will route us directly to the surface-level bus terminal."

"Thank you, Casey," my mother said dryly, shifting the weight of the bags on her shoulder. "Lead the way."

Cassandra didn't need any instructions. She rolled smoothly down the long, sloping ADA ramp, conserving her momentum. We followed her through the echoing, fluorescent-lit tunnel and emerged up into the bright afternoon heat at the ALX bus terminal.

"Bay D," Cassandra stated, locking her brakes near a concrete pillar. "The 28K."

When the massive Capital Transit bus pulled up and lowered its hydraulic suspension, Cassandra wheeled herself aboard, settling into the priority bay while Dad paid the fares. For the next hour, we rumbled through the dense, crawling suburban sprawl of Northern Virginia. Just as my mother had predicted, the traffic began to severely coagulate as we approached the infamous Seven Corners intersection. 

Mom stared out the window at the chaotic, multi-lane nightmare of cars trying to merge across US-50, her jaw tight. "I haven't missed this," she muttered.

Cassandra, however, watched the gridlock with cold, analytical satisfaction. "The bus has dedicated mass-transit mass. Smaller vehicles yield. The timeline is holding."

The 28K eventually broke free of the intersection, rumbling down toward US-29. The driver pulled over at the near side of Gallows Road, and the ramp hissed down to the pavement.

"Alright, dismount," Casey ordered, hopping off the bus. "We have to cross US-29. The 1C picks up on the far side."

We waited for the crosswalk signal, a small, heavily-laden family unit crossing the blistering asphalt of the highway. Once we secured our position at the far shelter, the 1C bus arrived within five minutes. We boarded, and it was a short, highly-efficient shot directly into the Dunn Loring rail station. 

Stepping into the Dunn Loring station offered a brief respite, trading the oppressive surface heat for the blasts of air-conditioning from the idling Orange Line train waiting in the median of I-66. Cassandra effortlessly swiped through the wide ADA faregate. We took the Orange Line for exactly one stop, disembarking at Vienna Station.

"Final transfer," Cassandra announced as we navigated the open-air concrete platform to the surface bus bays. "The CCT Green Line 1."

The CCT bus was smaller than the massive Capital Transit coaches, but it featured a flawless, low-floor boarding ramp. We climbed aboard, and the bus initiated its loop out of the Vienna transit center, rolling smoothly toward the George Wythe campus. 

When the bus pulled up to the curb outside our motel, we finally disembarked. The brutal humidity hit us again, but the massive, exhausting transfer was over. The surface fleet had delivered us right to the front door.

"I'll secure the reservations," Casey announced confidently, adjusting her backpack as she marched toward the motel lobby's glass doors. "I have the confirmation numbers memorized."

"That's very helpful, sweetie," Mom said, reaching out and gently snagging Casey by the shoulder strap of her backpack before she could push the doors open. "But I think your father and I will handle the check-in. They generally require a credit card and an adult with a driver's license."

Casey sighed, clearly annoyed by the bureaucratic red tape. "Fine. But request ground-floor adjacency. Cassandra cannot navigate a motel stairwell."

"We booked the accessible suite, Casey," Dad assured her, pulling the heavy glass door open for the family. "I promise, the logistics are secured. Let's just get into the air conditioning."

### Part 4: The Crown Burger Debrief – 6:30 PM

# Part 4: The Crown Burger Debrief – 6:30 PM

Once we unloaded our luggage into the adjoining motel rooms, my parents realized exactly how exhausted they were from the four-leg transit gauntlet. Rather than attempting to navigate a sit-down restaurant, we opted for the immediate, high-caloric efficiency of the Crown Burger located right next door in the same parking lot.

We pushed a couple of plastic tables together near the window. The air-conditioning in the fast-food dining room was blasting, providing welcome relief from the thick Virginia heat.

Casey was practically vibrating as she tore into the small cardboard box of her kids' meal, immediately extracting a cheap, plastic wind-up toy. At ten years old, I had finally decided I was too old for the kids' menu. I sat across from her, unwrapping my standard Crown Burger with a quiet sense of maturity. I felt like I was starting to outgrow the childish stuff, but as I watched Casey gleefully wind up her plastic car and send it skittering across the Formica table, I kept my mouth shut. I didn't want to spoil it for her. I wanted my kid sister to just enjoy being a kid.

"Alright, let's review the timeline for tomorrow," Casey announced between bites of French fries. She pulled a folded, slightly crumpled printed itinerary from her pocket and smoothed it out on the table. "Registration is at 0800 in the student union. The welcoming speech is at 0830. After that, we need to divide and conquer to maximize our coverage of the—"

"Casey, I appreciate the tactical support," Cassandra interrupted gently, placing her hand softly over our little sister's printed schedule. "But I have the logistics for tomorrow secured."

Casey blinked, looking up at her older sister. "You do?"

"I do," Cassandra nodded, taking a sip of her soda. "It's going to operate exactly like the VDU open house we attended last week. They're going to try and funnel everyone into massive, generalized campus tours. We are going to bypass the general tour and proceed directly to the Computer Science department. I need to verify their hardware infrastructure before I commit."

My father paused, wiping his hands with a napkin. "What exactly are you looking for, Cassandra? Doesn't every college have computers?"

"There is a massive discrepancy in departmental funding right now," Cassandra explained, her eyes lighting up with that intense, analytical fire she always got when discussing systems architecture. "The industry is shifting entirely toward object-oriented programming. C++ is the established standard, but the major Silicon Valley firms just announced a new cross-platform runtime architecture in May. The entire structure of the World Wide Web is about to fundamentally change."

Mom stared at her, completely lost. "Right. The... Web."

"If George Wythe University is still teaching archaic procedural languages on outdated mainframes, they are useless to me," Cassandra continued smoothly, ignoring the blank stares of our parents. "I need to see their advanced server labs. I need to verify that they have T1 internet backbone access for their undergraduates, not just the graduate researchers. With Quantum OS 95 launching next month, the home PC market is about to explode, and I refuse to spend four years at an institution that is technologically stagnant."

Casey nodded solemnly, despite definitely not understanding what a T1 backbone was. "So we bypass the general tour. We secure the advanced server lab."

"Exactly," Cassandra smiled, leaning back in her manual wheelchair and picking up a French fry. "Then, at 1300 hours, we meet with the disability counselor to inspect the freshman dormitories. I need to physically measure the desk clearance to ensure my custom desktop rig will fit. It has a heavy CRT monitor and a full-tower ATX case, so standard dorm furniture might require structural modification."

"I can measure the desks," Casey offered eagerly, winding up her plastic toy car again. "I'll bring the tape measure."

"You are officially assigned to measurement duty," Cassandra agreed with a serious nod, delegating the task perfectly to keep our little sister engaged. 

I took another bite of my burger, looking around the plastic tables of the Crown Burger. Tomorrow was going to be a massive, exhausting day of academic tours and ADA compliance checks. But as I watched Cassandra seamlessly manage Casey's energy and outline her ruthless technological requirements, I realized something. 

Cassandra didn't just want to attend college. She was planning to conquer it.

## Chapter 2: The Open House – Summer 1995

### Part 1: The Continental Breakfast – 7:30 AM

# Part 1: The Continental Breakfast – 7:30 AM

The George Wythe University Student Union was a massive, echoing atrium of brutalist concrete and large glass windows, designed to look impressive on brochures but currently functioning as a highly inefficient echo chamber. 

By 7:30 AM, the space was already swarming with nervous high school upperclassmen and their overly caffeinated parents. Tables were laden with massive stainless steel coffee urns, towers of slightly stale bagels, and sweating pitchers of orange juice. 

While my parents navigated the chaotic registration tables to collect Cassandra's printed itinerary and campus map, Casey immediately took tactical control of the dining area.

"Here," Casey ordered, dropping her brightly colored backpack onto a chair at a large circular table near the perimeter of the room. "This table has an unobstructed sightline to the podium, but it's positioned close enough to the exit that we won't get bottlenecked when the welcoming speech concludes. David, secure the perimeter. I am going to procure provisions."

I sat down at the table, suppressing a laugh. I was ten, and I was entirely content to let my eight-year-old sister run point on breakfast logistics. "I'll guard the backpacks, Casey. Get me a blueberry muffin if they have them."

A few minutes later, Cassandra rolled smoothly up to the table, locking her brakes. She had a campus map resting on her lap, but she barely glanced at it. She was already scanning the room, evaluating the crowd, the faculty standing near the walls, and the overall operational flow of the event. 

"The accessibility access to this building is acceptable," Cassandra noted quietly, picking up one of the brochures my mother had brought over. "The ramp grading was within standard tolerances, and the automatic doors functioned perfectly. However, the elevator bank is undersized for a student union of this capacity."

Mom sat down, looking exhausted before the day had even officially begun. Dad returned a moment later, carrying a tray loaded with coffee, bagels, and a massive plate of fruit. 

"I have secured the high-value items," Casey announced, marching back to the table with two blueberry muffins clutched in her small hands. She dropped one onto my napkin and took her seat next to Cassandra. 

As the family settled in to eat, a microphone screeched at the front of the room. A sharply dressed university administrator stepped up to the podium, adjusting the mic stand. 

"Welcome, prospective students and families, to George Wythe University!" the administrator's voice boomed, echoing harshly off the concrete walls. "We are thrilled to have you here today. GWU is an institution that prides itself on looking to the future..."

Cassandra quietly took a bite of her bagel, her eyes fixed on the administrator. She was listening to the speech, but I could tell her processor was already running completely different calculations. She wasn't here to be sold on the university's prestige or their marketing brochures. She was here to stress-test their infrastructure. 

"When this concludes," Cassandra leaned over and whispered to Casey, completely ignoring the booming speech echoing around us, "we bypass the guided walking tour entirely. We proceed straight to the academic quad. I need to see the Computer Science building."

Casey nodded, taking a bite of her muffin. "I have the route memorized. We move on your signal."

### Part 2: The Architecture of the Past – 9:45 AM

# Part 2: The Architecture of the Past – 9:45 AM

Despite Cassandra's earlier insistence on bypassing the general campus tour, she quickly realized that the George Wythe University campus layout was highly irregular. Because we lacked the necessary topographic data to navigate the winding footpaths and dense brick facades autonomously, she made the tactical decision to join the massive herd of prospective students for the guided academic walking tour. 

For the next hour, we slowly followed a painfully enthusiastic student ambassador walking backward and pointing out the library, the liberal arts buildings, and the various dining halls. Cassandra wheeled herself near the back of the group, conserving her energy and mentally mapping the concrete pathways, curb cuts, and elevator locations. 

When the tour finally wound its way past a low, squat, heavily-air-conditioned brick building, the guide pointed enthusiastically. "And right here is the Computer Science building, home to our advanced programming labs!"

Cassandra immediately locked her eyes on the entrance. She had the coordinates. 

When the tour eventually circled back and terminated at the student union, the massive crowd began to disperse toward various breakout sessions. Cassandra didn't hesitate. She pivoted her wheelchair sharply. "Breakaway," she ordered quietly. "We are heading back to the CS building."

Casey adjusted her backpack, marching faithfully beside her older sister's wheelchair as we navigated back across the quad. My parents followed, looking slightly bewildered as we officially went off-script.

We pushed through the heavy glass double doors of the Computer Science building, immediately hitting a wall of chilled, aggressively processed air. The lobby was lined with recruitment tables, manned by smiling faculty members handing out glossy pamphlets detailing the department's cutting-edge curriculum. 

Arranged on the tables were several massive desktop computer towers, their heavy CRT monitors humming with static electricity. 

Cassandra wheeled herself up to the closest display, adjusting her glasses as she inspected the screen. Casey leaned in right next to her, staring at the blocky, colorful interface. 

"Is this the new Quantum OS 95?" Casey whispered eagerly. 

"No," Cassandra replied, her voice perfectly flat. "It is Atlas OS/2."

A faculty member leaned over the table, smiling brightly. "That's right! We just completely overhauled the entire department's infrastructure. Every single machine in our student labs is now running Atlas OS/2, networked via a brand new Nexus NetShare topography. It is the absolute pinnacle of stable, 32-bit architecture."

Cassandra offered him a tight, incredibly polite smile. "It certainly is an investment."

She wheeled herself slowly down the line of tables, her processor working overtime. IBM—or rather, Atlas—had successfully convinced the university to commit their entire departmental budget to a platform that was practically already dead. With Quantum OS 95 dropping in a month, betting the farm on Atlas OS/2 was a catastrophic failure of market foresight. 

At the very end of the row of tables, Cassandra paused. Set up on a small pedestal was a thick, beige keyboard with a massive, blocky casing. A small handwritten sign next to it read: *Where it all began!*

It was an Apex 64. 

Cassandra let out a soft breath, reaching out and gently tapping the heavy plastic chassis of the vintage machine. She remembered the simpler times. She remembered sitting in front of a glowing television screen as a kid, watching the blinking cursor of the Apex 64, learning the absolute basics of logic and syntax. It was a beautiful machine. 

But times were changing. The industry was accelerating at a terrifying, exponential rate, and nostalgia wasn't going to secure a career in the coming tech boom. 

"Is something wrong, Cass?" I asked quietly, standing next to her wheelchair. 

Cassandra kept her polite, pleasant expression firmly in place for the benefit of the faculty watching her, but her eyes were cold as she looked at the rows of Atlas OS/2 machines. 

"This department is a museum, David," she murmured, her voice so low only Casey and I could hear it. "They just don't realize it yet."

### Part 3: The Dormitory Metric – 1:00 PM

# Part 3: The Dormitory Metric – 1:00 PM

We left the oppressive chill of the Computer Science building and made our way back across the academic quad to the Student Services center. At precisely 1300 hours, we were sitting in the small, warmly lit office of the George Wythe University Disability Services coordinator. 

The coordinator was a younger woman named Sarah. She was incredibly welcoming, pulling up campus housing blueprints on her desktop computer the moment Cassandra mentioned her desk measurements. 

"We absolutely have fully wheelchair-accessible dorms in the freshman housing blocks," Sarah assured her, turning her monitor so Cassandra could see the floor plan. "The standard suites have two Twin XL beds connecting to a second dorm room via a shared bathroom. In the accessible suites, the bathroom turning radius is expanded, the shower is a roll-in, and the desks are fully modular. We can adjust the desk height before you even arrive on move-in day."

"That is excellent," Cassandra nodded, offering a genuine smile. "I will be bringing a rather large desktop rig. It requires a heavy CRT monitor and a full-tower ATX case, so standard fixed-height dorm furniture is usually a critical failure point."

Sarah laughed, leaning back in her chair. "You're building your own rig? My brother is a computer science major out on the West Coast, and that's all he talks about." She glanced at the door before lowering her voice playfully. "He goes to Northwest Pacific University. His department has actually been beta testing the new Quantum OS 95 for months."

Cassandra sat up slightly straighter, her interest suddenly piquing. "NPU has beta access?"

"Oh, absolutely," Sarah nodded, smiling at the sudden intensity in my sister's eyes. "He said it's completely changing the way they network their labs. In fact, they just got their hands on an early developer build for Quantum's new enterprise networking software. I think they're calling the beta *Project Citadel*. It's supposed to be the 32-bit architecture that will eventually run all the university servers."

"Project Citadel," Cassandra repeated softly, committing the codename to memory. "What will the retail version be called?"

"I think he said Quantum NT 4.0," Sarah replied, shrugging. "I'm not a tech person myself, but he loves it. Which is why I always try to steer our tech-focused students carefully. Between you and me, I know our CS department here at GWU just invested heavily in Atlas OS/2."

"I saw," Cassandra said politely.

Sarah offered a sympathetic smile. "I don't have any say in what the academic departments do with their budgets, but even I know Atlas isn't the future. If you're building an ATX rig to run Quantum OS 95, you might be a little frustrated in our student labs."

Cassandra looked around the small office. The environment at GWU was undeniably inviting. The disability counselor was accommodating, the accessibility infrastructure was perfectly sound, and the campus was physically beautiful. She could very easily see herself living here, navigating the pathways and catching the CCT bus into Vienna. 

But as Casey eagerly pulled out her tape measure to double-check the desk dimensions on the blueprint, Cassandra remained quiet. She wasn't going to make her final decision today. She was going to wait. 

Because once Casey and I finished our summer camps at VDU, our family was flying to Seattle. And Cassandra suddenly needed to see exactly what Northwest Pacific University was doing with Project Citadel.

### Part 4: Early Dismissal – 2:15 PM

# Part 4: Early Dismissal – 2:15 PM

We stepped out of the Student Services center and back into the humid Virginia afternoon. The Open House was technically scheduled to run until five o'clock, with more breakout sessions, a student life panel, and an evening mixer in the dining hall. 

Cassandra, however, had seen enough. 

"The tactical objective has been achieved," Cassandra announced, locking her brakes near the campus transit shelter. "We have verified the accessibility tolerances of the housing blocks, mapped the core academic pathways, and assessed the technological infrastructure of the Computer Science department. There is no mathematical reason to remain here for the mixer."

My father looked at her, wiping sweat from his forehead. "Are you sure, Cass? We came all the way up here. We don't have to rush off."

"I have the data I need," Cassandra replied simply. She wasn't angry or disappointed. She was just ruthlessly efficient. Atlas OS/2 was a terminal diagnosis for the department's future, but the disability counselor's intel regarding Northwest Pacific University had just opened an entirely new strategic pathway. She was already mentally moving on to Seattle. "The CCT Gold Line 1 arrives in six minutes. It loops directly through the City of Fairfax."

Mom sighed in relief, clearly thrilled to be skipping a two-hour student panel in an un-air-conditioned lecture hall. "Fairfax it is. I think we've earned a break."

Casey looked down at her meticulously highlighted itinerary, visibly distressed by this sudden deviation from the schedule. "But the student life panel! We haven't investigated the extracurricular clubs!"

"You can investigate the ice cream options in historic downtown Fairfax, Casey," Dad offered smoothly, which instantly neutralized our little sister's objections. 

When the CCT bus pulled up, the ramp lowered, and we piled on. Instead of taking the loop back toward the Vienna Orange Line station, we rode it south into the heart of the City of Fairfax. 

The campus gave way to the historic downtown district, a charming cluster of old brick buildings, wide sidewalks, and sprawling oak trees. We disembarked near the old courthouse and spent the rest of the afternoon acting like a normal family on a summer vacation, completely detached from the high-stress logistics of college hunting.

We found a classic diner with checkered floors and vinyl booths. Dad ordered a massive plate of fries for the table, and Casey spent twenty minutes aggressively debating the mathematical superiority of chocolate milkshakes over vanilla. Cassandra sat by the window, sipping a soda and watching the traffic roll through the historic intersection. 

I sat across from her, dipping a fry in ketchup. "Are you disappointed?" I asked quietly, so Casey wouldn't hear and start another lecture. "About the computers?"

Cassandra looked away from the window, adjusting her glasses. The intense, calculating edge she had carried all morning was gone, replaced by a quiet sense of clarity. 

"No, David," she said softly. "It's better to find out now. A system is only as strong as its foundation. If you build a house on an obsolete architecture, it doesn't matter how nice the dorms are. It's still going to collapse."

She looked back out the window, watching a Capital Transit bus rumble past the diner. 

"We just have to find a school that's building for the future," she added. "Not the past."

### Part 5: The Evening Routine – 8:00 PM

# Part 5: The Evening Routine – 8:00 PM

By the time we navigated the CCT bus back to the motel in Vienna, the sun had set, and the oppressive Virginia heat had finally broken into a mild, humid evening. 

We retreated into our adjoining rooms. Dad collapsed onto one of the beds in my parents' room, grabbing the television remote, while Casey immediately unpacked her notebooks onto the small desk in our room, determined to mathematically graph the efficiency of the CCT bus loops we had just taken.

Cassandra wheeled herself to the center of our room, locking her brakes. The grueling transit rides and the long day of navigating the GWU campus had finally taken their toll. Her shoulders slumped slightly, and the sharp, unyielding energy that usually drove her had faded into physical exhaustion.

The adjoining door opened, and Mom stepped through. 

"Alright, Cass," Mom said softly, her tone shifting from the exasperated parent managing a college tour to the focused, quiet cadence she always used for the evening routine. "Let's get you ready for bed."

Casey immediately looked up from her notebooks, dropping her pencil. "I can assist," she volunteered eagerly, hopping off her chair. "I have analyzed the spatial dimensions of the accessible shower stall. If we utilize a pivot transfer—"

"No, Casey," Mom interrupted gently, placing a hand on our little sister's shoulder to stop her forward momentum. "Thank you, sweetheart, but Cassandra needs privacy. And this requires an adult."

Casey frowned, clearly offended that her logistical expertise was being rejected. "But I am part of the support team. I secured the breakfast perimeter. I measured the desk clearance."

"I know you did," Cassandra said, offering Casey a tired, affectionate smile. "And your data collection was flawless. But this isn't logistics, Casey. It's just my ADLs."

Activities of Daily Living. It was the clinical term Cassandra always used for the deeply personal, physically demanding reality of her condition—things like transferring, bathing, and changing clothes. 

Mom wheeled Cassandra into the large, accessible bathroom, pulling the heavy door shut behind them until it clicked, leaving Casey standing in the middle of the room, looking utterly defeated.

I walked over and pulled my eight-year-old sister into a hug. She was stiff for a second, still annoyed, before she finally sighed and leaned against me. 

"It's okay, Case," I told her quietly, resting my chin on top of her head. "You did a great job today. But sometimes, Cassandra just needs the adults to help her."

"It's inefficient," Casey mumbled against my shirt. "A properly integrated support team shouldn't have artificial age restrictions."

I smiled, letting her go so she could return to her transit graphs. But as I sat down on the edge of my Twin XL bed, listening to the muffled sounds of the running shower and Mom's quiet, encouraging voice guiding Cassandra through her transfers, I found myself staring at the closed bathroom door. 

Casey viewed our sister's life as a series of complex logistical equations to be solved. But for the first time, at ten years old, I was starting to recognize the profound physical vulnerability beneath Cassandra's brilliant, armored exterior. She relied on our parents to maintain her independence. 

A quiet thought sparked in the back of my mind. Our parents weren't going to be around forever to help her with her ADLs. What would happen when Cassandra went to college? Or when she got her own apartment? 

*What if it was me?* I thought to myself. *If it was just me and Cassandra... would I be strong enough to step in and help her?*

I didn't know the answer yet. But as I listened to the water running, I realized that I wanted to learn.

# Part 8: The GWU Rivalry and Deep Pressure – 9:00 PM

### Part 1: The Call – April 21st, 1996
