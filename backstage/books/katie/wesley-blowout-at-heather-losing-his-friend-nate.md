## Saturday, January 11, 2003, 10:40 AM — Five Red Crayon X's & The Only Boy Who Never Talked Baby-Talk

Up in his sunlit Chesapeake bedroom, fifteen-year-old Wesley Collins uncapped his thick red washable marker, stood right in front of the Puppy-a-Month wall calendar hanging beside his closet door, and drew a careful, triumphant diagonal X across the square for Friday, January tenth.

That left Saturday, January eleventh—the square with the shiny yellow smiley-face sticker his mom had helped him press onto the paper back on New Year's Day.

*Pretzel Saturday.*

Slipping his smooth solid-blue silicone sensory ring over his right wrist and giving the cool rubber two happy squeezes to settle the excited buzz in his fingers, Wesley checked himself in the dresser mirror the way his dad had taught him. Navy-blue fleece pullover zipped halfway up so the collar didn't rub his throat. Clean blue jeans. Both black sneaker laces double-knotted tight so neither loop could trip him on the mall tile.

Downstairs in the foyer, he heard the familiar jingle of Heather's brass keyring.

Wesley loved his big cousin Heather with his whole chest. At school in the self-contained wing, kids in the regular hallways either looked right through Wesley like he was part of the cinderblock wall or slowed their voices down into high, syrupy toddler voices—*Hiiii, Wes-ley!*—even though he was fifteen years old, taller than half the sophomores, and understood almost every single everyday word people said around him as long as they didn't talk three at once.

Nate Prairie had never once used that syrupy voice on him.

From the very first Saturday fourteen months ago when the glass elevator doors had slid open on the second floor of Emerald Creek Mall and two blonde sixteen-year-old twins had rolled out side by side in sleek black power wheelchairs, Nate had looked straight across at Wesley at eye level, held out his right fist, and said, *"Hey, Wes—glad you came. Want half the cinnamon nuggets?"* When the blender at the fruit-smoothie counter shrieked so loud Wesley had to clamp his forearm over his ear and hum low in his throat to keep his head from spinning, Nate hadn't stared or laughed; Nate had calmly reached sideways to hold his sister Katie's hand while Katie did the exact same quiet hum under her breath, and then Nate had slid the biggest, warmest cinnamon-sugar pretzel nugget straight onto Wesley's red tray.

Nate was Wesley's guy buddy. Not a teacher, not a classroom paraprofessional with a lanyard, not a grown-up uncle—Wesley's very first real teenage guy friend on the whole planet.

Eager to beat the eleven-o'clock line at Annie's Pretzels, Wesley bounded down the beige carpeted stairs four steps behind Heather, crossed the living room, and tapped the white front door twice with his flat palm—*pat, pat*—before turning around with his biggest grin and bumping his own knuckles together right in front of his sternum. *Let's go get Nate!*

Except Heather didn't walk to the front door.

Heather stopped halfway down the stairs. Her face looked pale and tight, the way people looked right before a thunderstorm knocked the cable television out, and her fingers stayed wrapped around her car keys instead of reaching for the brass deadbolt.

"Hey, Wes," Heather said in a strange, too-bright voice as she stepped slowly down onto the bottom step. "Listen, bud—you and I can still drive over to Greenbrier for French fries or look at the turtles at the pet shop on Battlefield Boulevard if you want, okay? Just the two of us today."

Wesley blinked. His smile slipped sideways. *Greenbrier?* Nate and Katie didn't go to Greenbrier; the blue-and-yellow bus with the hydraulic front lift dropped Nate and Katie off outside Emerald Creek Mall!

Thinking Heather had simply mixed up which Saturday square they were on, Wesley walked two steps over to the tall oak bookshelf between the front windows and pointed squarely at the wooden five-by-seven frame sitting on the middle shelf—his favorite object in the entire house—showing Katie, Nate, Heather, and Wesley smiling together beneath the white tented mall roof over four red Annie's Pretzels trays. Wesley tapped beside the frame twice, bumped his knuckles together again, and hummed a clear upward question toward Heather. *No, silly—Emerald Creek! Nate and Katie!*

Heather sat down hard on the bottom stair step and let go of her car keys.

"We... we aren't meeting Nate and Katie at Annie's Pretzels today, Wes," Heather said quietly, staring at her jeans before looking up at him. "Nate and I aren't boyfriend and girlfriend anymore. We broke up on Monday. So we won't be hanging out with Nate and Katie anymore."

At first, the words didn't fit together inside Wesley's head.

*Broke up on Monday.* Five whole days ago—all five days Wesley had been drawing red X's on his bedroom wall calendar waiting for Saturday!

*Won't be hanging out with Nate and Katie anymore.*

Wait. Not just today? *Anymore?*

A cold, dizzy drop opened right under Wesley's ribs—the exact terrifying feeling of stepping off a curb in the dark when the pavement wasn't where your sneaker expected it to be. Inside his chest, ten urgent words tried to rush up his throat at the exact same second—*Wait, Nate is MY friend too! Tell Nate I still want to come! Call Nate on the phone!*—and just like every single day of his life, the doorway between his brain and his tongue stayed locked shut, trapping every word behind his teeth where nobody could hear them.

And right behind that locked doorway came the realization that terrified him down to his bones:

*Wesley couldn't call Nate.*

If Wesley picked up the beige corded telephone on the kitchen wall and dialed a number, all the person on the other end would hear were his hums and breaths; strangers hung up when that happened. Wesley didn't know Nate's phone number anyway. Wesley didn't know Nate's street name in Virginia Beach. Wesley couldn't drive a car, didn't have a city bus pass, and wasn't allowed past the end of his own Chesapeake cul-de-sac without an adult or Heather beside him.

Heather was his bridge. Heather was the *only* way Nate even knew where Wesley was—and right now, Nate was probably sitting in his wheelchair in Virginia Beach thinking Wesley didn't want to eat pretzels with him anymore either, all because Heather had messed everything up!

The terror in Wesley's chest caught fire all at once, exploding upward into hot, roaring, helpless anger.

*"AAH—NO! HNN-GH!"*

Wesley flung his left arm straight out toward the closed front door, his throat burning as the loudest shout he had ever let out inside his mom's house echoed off the ceiling. He stayed rooted to his spot on the beige carpet six feet away from the stairs—he would never, ever touch or hurt Heather—yet every muscle in his shoulders shook as hot tears stung the corners of his eyes.

"Wes, wait—please don't yell, it wasn't my fault!" Heather cried from the step, holding both hands out as tears spilled down her cheeks. "Someone at school told everybody that Nate was at a bad party on New Year's Eve, and people in my homeroom were laughing at me, so I had to—"

*"UH-UH! NO!"* Wesley roared right back, shaking his head hard from side to side and stamping his right sneaker straight down into the carpet.

*Bad party? Nate?!* Even at fifteen without spoken sentences, Wesley knew garbage when he heard it! Nate didn't even like loud blenders at the food court! Nate drank ginger ale and apple juice and held Katie's hand whenever strangers bumped their wheelchairs! Heather had listened to mean kids at school instead of listening to *Nate*—and because Heather had messed up, Wesley had just lost his best friend!

Then a second jolt of ice-cold panic shot through Wesley's chest.

On the evening sitcoms Heather watched on television, whenever a teenage girl broke up with her boyfriend, she always marched over to her shelf and ripped up his pictures or threw them in the kitchen trash can.

Spinning toward the oak bookshelf before Heather could even stand up from the stairs, Wesley scooped the five-by-seven wooden frame clean off the middle shelf with both hands, turned the glass strictly inward against the soft navy fleece over his pounding heart so only the brown wooden backing and easel stand faced the room, wrapped his right arm and blue silicone ring protectively across it, and pointed his left hand one last time straight at the front door where Heather's car was parked.

"Wesley, honey, Heather would never touch your picture," his mom said softly and steadily from beside the bookcase, giving him plenty of breathing room. "That frame belongs to you, sweetheart. You can keep it safe in your room."

Wesley didn't trust anybody downstairs with Nate's picture today. Looking once more at Heather sitting small and crying on the bottom step—wanting her to see every bit of how badly she had broken his world—Wesley walked a wide arc around the far side of the stair rail, climbed the stairs two steps at a time with the oak frame pressed tight to his chest, kicked his bedroom door shut with a boom that shook the wall calendar, and sat down on the edge of his bed rocking slowly back and forth with both arms wrapped around his only surviving piece of Emerald Creek Mall.

He didn't know how many Saturdays it was going to take. All fifteen-year-old Wesley knew, setting that oak frame carefully beside his pillow where nobody could ever throw it away, was that he was going to carry that picture back downstairs and tap Nate's face in front of Heather every single weekend until she went back and fixed what she broke.
