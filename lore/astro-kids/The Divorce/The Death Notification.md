# Lore: The Dispatch TDD & The Winter Storm (Death Notification)

**Tags:** `#the_triplets` `#technology` `#valerie` `#timeline_2004` `#worldbuilding`

## The 2:00 PM Shutdown

By early afternoon on that Saturday in January 2004, the winter storm that was hammering the Shenandoah Valley with freezing sleet and slush had begun spilling over the Blue Ridge Mountains. Inside the Piedmont Central Branch library, Valerie was working her standard weekend shift, watching the snow rapidly accumulate on the downtown Lynchburg streets.

At exactly 2:00 PM, the City of Lynchburg made an executive call: the roads were becoming dangerously slick, and all municipal buildings, including the library, would shut down early at 3:00 PM. Valerie and the front desk staff immediately began ushering patrons out and rushing through their closing duties to beat the weather.

## The 2:32 PM Direct Dial Failure

High up on the mountain at the Big Island cabin, Lyra, Orion, and Celeste were executing their Latchkey Protocol. At 2:32 PM, the landline rang.

The Caller ID displayed an unfamiliar 540 number—local to the Shenandoah Valley region. On the other end was a Virginia State Trooper standing on the side of Interstate 81 Southbound in the freezing rain, surrounded by a catastrophic, multi-vehicle pileup. He had just pulled the cabin's phone number from the glovebox registration of the crushed vehicles.

Because she expected a friendly local caller, Lyra answered and opened the line with her standard audio signature: a sharp, rhythmic series of staccato tongue-clicks.

"Hello? Is this the residence of Valerie Miller?" the trooper asked over the sound of the highway. "I need to speak to an adult regarding a traffic incident."

Lyra, realizing this was official business but unable to speak, defaulted to her only tool. She dropped the handset onto the TTY's acoustic couplers and rapidly typed: `USE 711 PLEASE`.

The trooper, who had dialed directly from his radio pack and did not know the family used a TTY system, was suddenly blasted with the deafening, high-pitched screech of raw Baudot tones. Assuming the crash victims had a dedicated fax line, he hung up the phone.

Following protocol, the trooper radioed his regional dispatch center in Salem: _"Dispatch, I'm trying to make a next-of-kin notification for the I-81 fatalities, but the residential line sounds like a fax machine or a TDD."_

## The 2:37 PM Dispatch (The Relay)

Because 911 dispatch centers were federally mandated by the Americans with Disabilities Act to maintain and operate TDD equipment, the Salem dispatcher immediately switched their console over to the police TTY line and redialed the cabin.

At 2:37 PM, the landline rang again.

When Lyra placed the handset onto the acoustic couplers and hit the **Printer ON** toggle, the TTY machine handshook directly with the Virginia State Police dispatch center in Salem.

The thermal paper spooled upward with a stark, official transmission:

Plaintext

```
VA STATE POLICE DISPATCH
REGARDING I-81 TRAFFIC INCIDENT
IS VALERIE MILLER AVAILABLE GA
```

Lyra, recognizing immediately that this was an official emergency and not a telemarketer or a divorce lawyer, did not ask for details. She operated with her signature procedural efficiency, typing back a direct reroute:

Plaintext

```
SHE IS AT WORK
CALL 540-555-0199
PIEDMONT CENTRAL LIBRARY
ASK FOR VALERIE GA
SK
```

## The Library Notification

Minutes later, the heavy plastic phone on the Piedmont Branch circulation desk rang. Valerie, busy pulling cash from the till for the early closure, picked it up.

"Piedmont Central Branch, this is Valerie."

"Ms. Miller, this is the Virginia State Police. We were just routed to this number by a TDD user at your residence. I am calling to notify you of a catastrophic, multi-vehicle pileup on Interstate 81 Southbound."

Standing behind the circulation desk, Valerie listened as the trooper explained that both parents had been killed instantly in the twisted wreckage. The divorce, the threats of adult guardianship, the years of weaponized litigation—all of it had ended against the icy grill of a semi-truck.

Valerie hung up the phone. She stood in silence for a moment, the weight of a two-year siege instantly lifting off her shoulders. She walked into the back office and told the Lead Circulation Supervisor exactly what had just happened on the interstate.

Her boss didn't hesitate. "Valerie, clock out right now. Leave the tills, I will balance them. Get on the road before 3:00 PM—US-501 Northbound is a mountain pass and it's already freezing over. Go home to your family."

## The Mountain Pass & The Reunion

Valerie grabbed her coat, locked up the beat-up van, and began the treacherous, slow crawl up US-501 North toward Big Island. The drive, usually 35 minutes, took nearly an hour in the mounting snow. Her only fear during the drive was the van sliding off the mountain; she felt absolutely zero grief for the two people who had died.

When she finally pulled up to the cabin and unlocked the heavy front door, the triplets were waiting for her at the wooden table.

They weren't crying. They weren't mourning the parents who had viewed them purely as an infuriating burden and fought over them out of blinding spite. In fact, when Valerie officially broke the news, the immediate emotion radiating through the cabin was profound, overwhelming relief.

The triplets were simply thrilled that Valerie had made it up the icy mountain pass safely. There would be no more 757 area code phone calls. There would be no Guardianship hearings. The massive defense binder Valerie had built was completely useless. They spent the rest of the snowed-in Saturday exactly as they always did—together, safe, and finally, permanently free.
